Prospecting Motion

"Happy to walk you through it — this is something I've actually built from scratch, so I'll give you the real version, not the textbook one.
My motion has four stages: research, prioritise, sequence, and iterate.
First — research and account selection. Before I touch any outreach, I build a target list based on two things: how likely the company is to have the pain I solve, and how much the deal is worth if it lands. At Figma that meant looking for companies scaling design teams or going through digital transformation. For OpenAI it'd be AI-readiness signals — companies publicly investing in AI, hiring for AI roles, in industries where the use case is obvious. I'd rather spend an extra hour on research than blast a generic list.
Second — prioritise ruthlessly. This is a lesson I learned the hard way at Airwallex. Early on I chased every account and got stretched thin. Now I tier my list — tier one gets deep, personalised, multi-threaded outreach, tier two and three get lighter touches. Not every account deserves the same effort, and pretending they do is how you burn time.
Third — sequence across channels. I don't rely on one channel. My cadence is usually a mix of email, LinkedIn, and phone, spread over about two to three weeks. But the key thing for me is that every touch leads with a specific insight about their business — not 'I'd love to show you our product.' At Datadog I was averaging around nineteen new meetings a month doing this, and the meetings that converted were always the ones where I'd clearly done my homework on the prospect.
The messaging philosophy underneath all of it is problem-first, not product-first. I'm trained in Command of the Message and MEDDICC, so I'm always trying to lead with the prospect's Before State — the pain they're living with — rather than our features. The goal of a first touch isn't to pitch. It's to make the person think 'this person actually understands my world.'
And fourth — iterate. I treat my own outreach like an experiment. I track what subject lines get opens, what angles get replies, what falls flat — and I adjust weekly. When I built the Figma APAC playbook, that's exactly how I figured out what worked here versus what the North America team was doing — constant testing and refining until I had something repeatable.
So that's the motion — research, prioritise, sequence, iterate. Disciplined at the top, personalised where it counts, and always measured so I can improve it."

"How many touches before you give up on an account?"
"Depends on the tier. For a tier-one strategic account, I'll run a full multi-channel sequence over a few weeks, then put them into a longer-term nurture rather than fully dropping them — timing is often the reason for no response, not lack of interest. For lower tiers, I'm more disciplined about cutting and moving on. The mistake I see is people either giving up after two emails or stubbornly chasing dead accounts forever. The skill is knowing which is which."
"What does a good first-touch email look like to you?"
"Short. Specific. Built around them, not me. I open with something that proves I've done my homework — a recent announcement, a strategic move, a problem I can infer from their situation. Then I name the gap I think they have, not the product I'm selling. And I end with a low-friction ask — usually a question, not a meeting request. The whole thing should be readable in fifteen seconds and make them think 'huh, this person gets it.'"
"How do you use AI in your own prospecting?"
"Increasingly, a lot. I built that Glean agent to automate my pipeline reporting so I get time back for actual selling. I use ChatGPT for research synthesis — pulling together what a company's been announcing, what their priorities seem to be — and for drafting and sharpening outreach angles. But I'm careful not to let it make my outreach generic. AI helps me prepare faster; it doesn't replace the personalisation. If anything, it lets me personalise more accounts in the same amount of time."
"How do you handle a prospect who responds but isn't interested?"
"I try to learn from it rather than just move on. A reply, even a no, is a signal. I'll ask a light follow-up to understand why — wrong timing, wrong person, no budget, no pain. Sometimes it redirects me to the right person internally. Sometimes it tells me my targeting was off. Either way it's information I'd rather have than not."


Who will you target

It depends on which motion you're running, because OpenAI sells a few different things to a few different buyers. Broadly, I see three buying motions, and each has a different persona.
The first is the org-wide productivity motion — selling something like ChatGPT Enterprise across a whole company. For that, the economic buyer usually sits in the C-suite or one level below — a Chief Operating Officer, a Chief Transformation or Digital Officer. But the people who actually feel the pain and become your champions are often function heads: a VP of Customer Support drowning in tickets, a Head of Operations trying to cut manual work, a Revenue Operations leader. They're the ones who carry the message up.
The second is the builder motion — companies that want to build AI into their own products using the API. There, you're talking to technical buyers: a CTO, a VP of Engineering, a Head of Product, a Head of Data Science. At startups and scale-ups, often the founders directly. The conversation is completely different — it's about capabilities, latency, model performance, cost per token.
The third is the governance and deployment motion — the newer enterprise layer, things like Frontier and agents. That's where you're engaging the most senior technical and strategic leaders: CTOs, CIOs, Chief AI Officers — people thinking about how to deploy and govern AI across the entire organisation safely.
Where I think the real skill comes in — and this is the part I'd focus on as a BDR — is identifying the champion before the economic buyer. The decision-maker rarely takes a cold outreach. But the VP of Support who's measured on resolution time, or the Head of Ops trying to do more with the same headcount — they feel the pain personally and they'll take the meeting. That's who I'd target first, then build upward toward the economic buyer.
And in APAC specifically, the buying committees tend to be more hierarchical and top-down than in the US, so understanding who has real authority versus who's an influencer matters a lot. That's something I've navigated at both Figma and Airwallex.


Competitors

The main competitors I see are Anthropic, Google, and Microsoft — with Meta and the open-source models competing more on the cost end.
Anthropic with Claude is the most serious enterprise competitor right now. They went enterprise-first from day one, they're strong in coding and document-heavy, compliance-sensitive workflows, and they've actually been winning meaningful business adoption share. I don't think it's useful to pretend otherwise.
Google has the strongest distribution — Gemini is built into Workspace, Search, Android — and serious compute. But their enterprise execution has been inconsistent because their attention is split across a huge consumer empire.
And Microsoft is the interesting one — historically OpenAI's biggest partner, now increasingly a competitor as that relationship evolves.
So how do we win? I don't think the answer is 'our model is better' — at the frontier, the models are all genuinely strong, and that's a race that goes back and forth every few months. I think we win on three things.
First, breadth and the consumer brand. OpenAI is the broadest platform — voice, image, video with Sora, Codex for engineering, all in one ecosystem. And ChatGPT is synonymous with AI for most employees already. That lowers adoption friction enormously — when the workforce already knows the tool, enterprise rollout is faster.
Second, the developer ecosystem. OpenAI has the deepest developer mindshare, which compounds — the integrations, the community knowledge, the default-choice effect. For companies building AI into their own products, that gravity matters.
But third — and this is the part I'd actually own as a BDR — we win on execution and trust at the human level. When the models are this close, the deal is often won or lost on which company's team understood the customer's business best, moved fastest, and earned the most trust. That's not a product feature. That's us. That's the seller. And that's the part I can directly control.


What do you know about AI?

"Honestly, I'll give you my high-level perspective — I'm not going to pretend I understand the technical architecture of how transformers work. 
The way I see it, AI is moving through three rough waves.
The first wave was AI as a productivity tool. ChatGPT, Copilot, Claude — individual users getting drafts written, code generated, ideas explored. That's still happening, and it's where most enterprise adoption starts.
The second wave — which we're in right now — is AI as an embedded layer. Companies aren't just letting employees use ChatGPT; they're integrating models into their own products and workflows through APIs. That's where things get interesting commercially, because it's no longer a productivity story — it's a competitive differentiation story.
The third wave, which OpenAI is clearly pushing toward with products like Frontier and Workspace Agents, is agentic AI. Systems that don't just respond to prompts but actually take actions — researching, writing, executing tasks across multiple tools autonomously. That's the layer that will reshape how organisations actually run.
What this means for sales — and the reason I'm excited about this role — is that the conversation with enterprise buyers is shifting. It's no longer 'do you want to use AI?' It's 'how does your company want to compete in a world where every competitor has access to the same models?' The differentiator is who deploys it most intelligently, with the right governance and the right business outcomes in mind.
