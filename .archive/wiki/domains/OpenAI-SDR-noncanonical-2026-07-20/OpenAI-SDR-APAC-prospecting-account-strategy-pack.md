## Core Prospecting Motion

### 15-Second Answer

> I would run prospecting as account strategy, not message volume. Start with the finite account universe, turn it into a named target list, tier by fit and readiness, test whether the signal is actually messageable, map the buying committee, and use every touch to sharpen the account thesis for the AD.

### 60-Second Answer

> The way I would run it is TAM, thesis, signal, stakeholder, message, learn. First, I would align with the AD on the account universe: financial services, life sciences, retail, regional platforms, and large regulated enterprises in APAC. Then I would turn that into a named account list and tier it: tier one for deep account strategy, tier two for targeted sequences, and tier three for monitor or nurture.
>
> For each account, I would separate first-party signals if available, second-party signals like events, partners, warm paths, or peer references, and third-party signals like AI hiring, public AI strategy, governance initiatives, developer activity, competitor usage, or customer-operations pressure. But the key test is messageability. I am not just asking, "Is this company interested in AI?" I am asking: can I connect the signal to a person, a workflow, a business pressure, a proof point, and a respectful reason to engage now?
>
> For the best accounts, I would map who has to believe, approve, defend, implement, and use the workflow. Then the outreach is problem-first, not product-first. After every reply, no-reply, objection, or meeting, I would update the account thesis and hand the AD the real state: why this account, why now, who matters, what proof is missing, and what next step is credible.

### 2-Minute Answer

> I think about strategic prospecting as finite-TAM stewardship. In a role like this, a bad generic touch is expensive because it can burn trust or put a strategic buyer on cooldown. So the goal is not to contact everyone. The goal is to know the territory well enough to identify which accounts deserve attention now, which should be monitored, and which should be left alone until the signal is stronger.
>
> I would start by mapping the account universe with the AD. For OpenAI APAC, that would mean regulated financial services, healthcare and life sciences, retail, digital platforms, regional headquarters, and large enterprises where AI could matter to knowledge work, customer operations, developer productivity, or governed deployment. Then I would turn that universe into a named account list with tiering, exclusions, and a clear reason each account belongs there.
>
> Then I would build the account thesis. What is the business priority? What workflow could AI actually change? Who owns that workflow? Where is the source of truth? What risk would block adoption? What proof would make the buyer comfortable moving from curiosity to a pilot? That is especially important for OpenAI because you cannot qualify a serious enterprise AI opportunity just by asking whether someone is "interested in AI." You have to understand where the use case sits in the business.
>
> From there, I would look for signals in three lanes. First-party signals if OpenAI makes them available: product usage, inbound interest, event attendance, developer experimentation, or pilot activity. Second-party signals: partner context, warm paths, customer references, events, alumni moves, or peer-company proof. Third-party signals: public AI strategy, AI or data hiring, governance programs, annual-report language, customer-support pressure, developer activity, competitor usage, or regulatory/compliance pressure.
>
> But I would separate true signals from messageable signals. A signal can be real and still not be a good reason to send outbound. The question is whether I can connect it to a named person or stakeholder group, a workflow hypothesis, a business pressure, a proof point close to their world, and a respectful reason to talk now. If not, it belongs in nurture or monitoring rather than a forced message.
>
> The account map then becomes the selling system: champion, business owner, technical evaluator, security or governance, finance/procurement/legal, executive sponsor, end users, and potential blockers. The first message should be short enough to understand in 15 seconds and specific enough that the buyer feels I did the work.
>
> The Salescraft proof is that I have built versions of this before. The useful work was not "AI writes more emails." It was mapping sources, scoring evidence, ranking accounts, identifying the best next seller action, and routing that into CRM or a seller handoff with human review. I would bring that same operating habit here: evidence to account thesis, account thesis to message, message to discovery, discovery to an AD-ready opportunity.

## Creating A Prospecting Playbook For OpenAI

Use this if asked: "How would you approach creating a prospecting playbook?"

### Short Answer

> The way I would build a prospecting playbook is the way I learned outbound at Airwallex: start with a market thesis, get close enough to the customer to understand the workflow, then turn what you learn into rules the team can repeat.
>
> I would not begin with "which companies are interested in AI?" That is too broad. I would pick a narrow wedge, learn the market manually, define the account universe, separate baseline ICP from best-case timing signals, tier the accounts, test the message by hand, map the buyer committee, and then build the operating cadence with the AD.
>
> The important thing is that a playbook is not just sequences. It is a living system: account rules, signals, proof points, stakeholder maps, messaging angles, discovery questions, CRM notes, AD handoff standards, and a feedback loop from every reply, meeting, objection, and closed-lost.

### Airwallex Example

> At Airwallex, I was the second outbound hire in Singapore. When I joined, outbound was still a small part of the revenue mix. A lot of revenue came from paid marketing and channel partnerships, but some of those deals were lower quality: smaller brands, weaker retention, or compliance risk that could create offboarding exposure. My job was to help outbound bring in stronger, more recognizable Singapore logos, but there was not yet a neat territory playbook.
>
> The bet I made was travel. I did the unglamorous work: cold calling, walking into offices, and learning the industry properly. One very practical thing I learned was that roughly 90% of the travel agencies I cared about were clustered near People's Park. Once I closed one or two brands, I would chat with people in the industry, figure out who the biggest players were, then literally walk over to their offices and knock on the door. That turned the market from an abstract list into a physical map.
>
> That fieldwork taught me why the pain was real. Travel agencies had very specific cross-border payment issues. They often had to pay local tour guides or tour leaders overseas on short timelines, because the gap between a booked tour and the actual tour date could be very tight. If the guide did not receive money on time and the group was already in-country, the stress was immediate.
>
> The economics were also specific. A single trip could involve payments to many guides on the ground, and each bank transfer could carry meaningful fees. Travel was also extremely seasonal. A huge share of transactions clustered around June and December, so they cared not only about price, but whether their provider would be responsive and alert during peak-volume windows when payments, checks, or operational issues could not wait.
>
> One more layer was corridor complexity. Because these agencies were dealing with tours, some destinations could be more exotic or compliance-sensitive. They would sometimes run into issues sending money into China, Russia, or Central Asia. China was a strong example for us because Airwallex's China payment rails were well integrated and we were licensed there. For the more sensitive corridors, I would check internally with compliance and get the customer a practical checklist of documents to have ready: source of funds, reason for transfer, invoices, and other supporting documents. Then, when those payments came through, I would flag them internally so they could be reviewed quickly during peak operating windows.
>
> The product wedge was not "move everything to Airwallex on day one." That would have felt too risky because banks were familiar and trusted. The wedge was the highest-pain workflow: overseas payouts for outbound tours, especially when timing, fees, corridor complexity, and reconciliation were painful.
>
> Risk removal came through a small next step. Instead of asking them to move everything at once, we would start with smaller transactions first, monitor them closely, and show that Airwallex delivered as sold. Once I could prove that Airwallex was legitimate, faster, easier to reconcile, and useful for the corridors they cared about, the conversation could expand into natural adjacencies: employee cards for tour guides, cleaner expense reconciliation, website checkout, and offline PoS. In the end, the deal became a bundle that created stickiness and vendor lock-in.
>
> That is what made the message stick. I was not pitching "better fintech infrastructure" in the abstract. I could talk about last-minute tour payments, overseas guide payouts, seasonal transaction spikes, transfer fees, and operational support during peak travel windows. Because I understood the workflow, I could sound like I had done the homework. Over time, that created a reputation in the travel vertical and made the outbound motion compound.

### OpenAI Translation

> I would approach OpenAI the same way. Pick a narrow vertical or workflow and learn it deeply enough that the outreach feels native. Customer operations in a regional platform is different from legal review in a bank, developer productivity in a software company, claims review in insurance, or research/reporting inside a conglomerate.
>
> For each wedge, I would want to know: what work is repetitive but judgment-heavy, where people are opening ten tabs today, what system is the source of truth, what risk stops automation, what metric matters, what proof would make the buyer comfortable, and which stakeholder would look good if the pilot worked. The best first wedge is the workflow with clear before/after proof: something the buyer can name as painful, approve as a small pilot, and recognize as improved quickly.
>
> Then I would build the playbook with the AD: define the account universe, tier it, identify baseline ICP and best-case signals, map the buyer committee, test the message manually, capture objections, and turn the pattern into account rules, discovery prompts, proof points, and handoff notes. AI would help me move faster, but the actual sales judgment is still choosing the right wedge, the right account, and the right reason to talk.
>
> Doing this unconventionally is not easy. You face rejection externally and sometimes internally because the thesis may not look obvious at first. But that is part of the job. Be creative, hands-on, persistent, and willing to jump through obstacles. The goal is not to run the most polished campaign on day one. The goal is to find the account wedge that is true enough, urgent enough, and differentiated enough to become a repeatable OpenAI playbook.

### Playbook Structure

| Layer | Question | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Market thesis | What is changing that makes AI urgent now? | Industry POV and reason the timing matters. |
| Workflow wedge | Which workflow is painful, repeated, high-value, and realistic to pilot? | Narrow use case and success metric. |
| Account universe | Which companies have the size, readiness, data, governance, and business pressure? | Named account list with tiers and exclusions. |
| Buyer map | Who owns workflow, budget, risk, technical validation, governance, and daily use? | Stakeholder map and proof gaps. |

| Signal threshold | Which signals are messageable, not just interesting? | Go / nurture / monitor decision. |

| Learning loop | What updates the playbook? | Objections, replies, lost accounts, closed opportunities, and buyer language. |

### Doctrine To Remember

- Do not import a playbook before diagnosing the market.
- Separate baseline ICP from best-case timing signals.
- A good playbook should say who not to pursue, not just who to pursue.
- Tier accounts so the best accounts get the most human attention.
- A signal can be true but not messageable.
- Finite TAM means every generic touch spends trust.
- Territory quality beats territory sprawl.
- The best first wedge is the workflow with clear before/after proof.
- Make the next step small enough for the buyer to trust.
- Proof has to feel close to the buyer's region, workflow, role, risk model, and business pressure.
- Founder-style market learning matters before automation: buyer language, objections, demo failure modes, and bad-fit patterns.
- AI should increase judgment density, not message volume.
- The playbook should preserve deal craft: account-specific evidence, warm paths, buyer co-authorship, proof gaps, human review, and AD-ready handoff notes.

## Account Prioritization Scorecard

Use this internally to answer "which accounts deserve deep research?"

### Fit Frame

> The sharper way I think about fit is that companies are not the unit of qualification. Workflows are. A bank, retailer, or airline can be a great logo but still be a weak opportunity if the workflow is vague, low-value, or impossible to move.
>
> So I would qualify fit at the workflow level: is the problem valuable, is OpenAI specifically relevant, can the company actually act, is there a reason now, and do we have a path to the people who can move it forward?
>
> The shorthand is value, readiness, and timing. But the more useful version is: workflow value, OpenAI fit, ability to act, reason now, and path to power. "AI is important" is not a compelling event; a product launch, cost pressure, compliance change, support-volume spike, developer backlog, expansion market, competitive pressure, or executive mandate is much stronger.
>
> OpenAI fit should answer a ladder question: which part of the OpenAI surface is this workflow ready for? ChatGPT Enterprise or Business for governed employee work, Codex for engineering work, Workspace Agents for repeatable team SOPs, API/tools/agents for custom builds, or Frontier/deployment support for large production-agent transformation.
>
> Then I would separate demand capture from demand creation. Some accounts already show demand through usage, inbound interest, hiring, public AI goals, events, pilots, or competitor evaluations. Those should be qualified quickly. Others may have a valuable workflow but weak ability to act, weak reason now, or no path to power yet. Those accounts should be educated, monitored, and nurtured until there is a real reason to engage.

| Dimension | Strong signal | Weak signal | Interview phrasing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Workflow value | Clear workflow bottleneck, support volume, regulated workflow pressure, developer backlog, expensive manual process, revenue impact, or customer experience impact. | Generic "AI could help" idea. | "I would prioritize where the business cost is visible." |
| OpenAI fit / ladder | Clear match to an OpenAI surface: ChatGPT Enterprise or Business for governed employee work, Codex for engineering workflows, Workspace Agents for shared repeatable SOPs, API/tools/agents for embedded builds, or Frontier/deployment for strategic production-agent transformation. | "AI could help" but no clear OpenAI surface, or simple deterministic automation better served by existing tools. | "I would first ask which rung this workflow belongs on." |
| Ability to act | AI strategy, product/API usage, AI hiring, developer activity, technical champion, data source, governance owner, current pilot, security/procurement path. | No owner, no data access, no technical path, no governance owner. | "A signal can be true but not yet actionable." |
| Reason now | Product launch, cost pressure, compliance change, support-volume spike, developer backlog, expansion market, competitive pressure, executive mandate. | "AI is important" with no reason to act now. | "Generic urgency is not enough; I want a reason this quarter matters." |
| Path to power | Plausible champion, workflow owner, executive sponsor, technical validator, governance/security contact, or warm path. | Only generic inbox, curious user, or unknown owner. | "For strategic accounts, the first person matters because they shape the internal path." |
| Proof proximity | Similar region, workflow, risk model, or buyer role. | Shiny but distant logo proof. | "The buyer should feel: that is basically us." |
| AD value | Handoff would include business issue, stakeholder map, use case, timing, competitor context, next step. | Only a curious user. | "The handoff should make the AD smarter, not busier." |

## Messageability

### Plain Definition

> A signal can be true but not messageable. A company can hire AI engineers, attend an event, publish an AI strategy, or use a competitor, but if I cannot connect that signal to a person, workflow, business pressure, proof point, and respectful reason to engage, it may belong in nurture rather than outbound.

### Checklist

- Is the account actually in the ICP for OpenAI's enterprise or builder motion?
- Is the signal recent enough to imply timing?
- Is there a named person or at least a clear stakeholder group?
- Is there a workflow hypothesis, not just "AI interest"?
- Is there a business pressure behind the workflow?
- Is there a proof point close to their world: region, role, risk model, workflow, or industry?
- Is there a respectful reason to engage now?
- Can the first message be understood in 15 seconds?
- Would I be comfortable explaining the source of the insight if the buyer asked?
- If the answer is no, should this account be monitored instead of contacted?

## Messageability Rules

Use this to keep the answer practical rather than buzzwordy.

| Rule | How Seth says it | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Warm path before cold path | "For a top account, I first look for trusted paths, shared context, prior workflow relevance, events, or peer-company proof before pure cold." | Finite-TAM accounts should not be burned with generic touches. |
| Signal is not automatically copy | "A signal can be true but not yet messageable." | Avoids creepy outreach and false urgency. |
| Use the topic, not the surveillance | "If someone engages with AI governance content, I would not say I saw the engagement. I would start a normal conversation about AI governance priorities." | Keeps social and intent signals respectful. |
| Strong proof must feel proximate | "The proof point should match the buyer's region, workflow, risk model, and role." | A buyer should feel "that is basically us," not "nice logo." |
| Top accounts need patience and speed | "I slow down until the reason to reach out is strong, then move fast once the buyer engages." | Protects trust while preserving momentum. |
| Every touch should teach the system | "Replies, no-replies, objections, wrong-person notes, and referrals all update the account thesis." | Turns outbound into account intelligence, not one-way sending. |

## APAC Nuance Answer

### 45-Second Answer

> APAC is not one market; each country requires its own approach. I learned that at Boxo, where I worked across APAC selling to or partnering with major fintech and wallet platforms in different markets: MoMo and Zalo or ZaloPay in Vietnam, Touch 'n Go in Malaysia, DANA in Indonesia, TrueMoney in Thailand, GCash in the Philippines, and others. The biggest lesson was that product-market fit and sales motion do not travel cleanly from one market to another.
>
> Singapore and ANZ may be stronger for regulated enterprise readiness, regional HQ conversations, and governance-heavy buyers. In parts of SEA, the motion can be more relationship-driven. Vietnam, for example, can have very strong young technical talent, but senior-buyer access and channel strategy may require more local nuance because of language and local-network barriers. The Philippines is very strong on English and operational talent, but because labor is comparatively available, the value case for software sometimes needs to be especially concrete around generating more revenue or reducing painful errors, not just lower-cost automation. Indonesia is too large to ignore, but the current market-confidence stress changes the tone: I would be careful not to sell AI as shiny transformation spend, and instead lead with governed productivity, customer operations, compliance/risk workflows, and measurable ROI for resilient banks, telcos, platforms, and conglomerates.
>
> But the bigger point is the adoption curve. In the US, for some categories, you might be selling after the category has hit product-market fit and the market is already aware. So the pitch can sound like "we are the best platform for X." In APAC, you may be earlier. You are not always selling to the majority yet; you are recruiting the first innovators and using that to build an APAC-specific reference base. Early adopters can be rarer, trust cycles are long, and references travel slowly.
>
> So I would not simply copy a US enterprise playbook. I would match the account strategy to buyer readiness to innovate. The sharper APAC motion is: "We are new here, but we have proof in the US. We are looking for the first few innovators in this region that want to define what AI-enabled work looks like in their industry." And I would focus a lot on trust: acting as a trusted advisor over time, asking good questions, and understanding the problems keeping that specific company up at night.

## Touch And Nurture Strategy

### Interview Answer

> I would not use one fixed number for every account. A good first call should answer: did we learn the business pain, confirm OpenAI relevance, identify who else is affected, understand what proof would make the buyer comfortable, and earn a concrete next step?
>
> I would show up with a point of view and account research, but I would not turn the call into a checklist. I do not need to force every MEDDICC field into the first conversation. The goal is to have a mutual business conversation and learn whether there is real pain where OpenAI can help.
>
> For OpenAI specifically, I would try to move from abstract AI interest into a specific workflow: what work is being done today, who owns it, what systems contain the truth, where the friction or risk is, what success would look like, and who would need to be involved before the company could seriously evaluate a pilot or deployment.

### Call Structure

1. **Start with the account thesis.** "I saw this signal, and my hypothesis is that it may connect to this workflow or priority. Is that actually relevant, or am I off?"
2. **Understand the current workflow.** How does the work get done today? Who touches it? What tools or systems are involved? Where does context get lost?
3. **Find the business gap.** What is slow, expensive, risky, manual, inconsistent, or hard to scale? What happens if nothing changes?
4. **Test urgency and timing.** Why now? Is there an executive priority, cost pressure, backlog, compliance concern, support issue, or AI initiative behind this?
5. **Ask what proof would matter.** "What would you need to see to believe this could work in your environment?" This is better than jumping into a generic demo.
6. **Map stakeholders lightly.** Who owns the workflow? Who uses it? Who approves risk? Who would block it? Who would look good if it worked?
7. **Recap and confirm.** "So it sounds like you want to fix this because [reason], the gap is [gap], and the proof you would need is [proof]. Did I get that right?"
8. **Earn the next step.** The next step should be specific: technical discovery, workflow scoping, AD follow-up, security/governance conversation, pilot discussion, or a reason to disqualify.

### What I Would Avoid

- Turning the first call into a rigid qualification checklist.
- Asking "are you interested in AI?" and treating that as qualification.
- Pitching every OpenAI product surface before understanding the workflow.
- Running a group demo too early.
- Ending with vague next steps like "send me more info."
- Handing the AD a meeting with no account story.

## How To Start A Customer Conversation

This is the process-briefing structure converted into speakable form.

> I would start with the customer's priorities, not the product. First, what is the business outcome they care about? Second, what is the current setup?
