## Interview Target

These cards must prove four things:

1. Seth can walk through a deal end to end without rambling.
2. Seth sells consultatively: customer priority, workflow pain, stakeholder risk, value driver, and business outcome first.
3. Seth can speak metrics cleanly while being honest about smaller or mid-market deal scope.
4. Seth has learned from each chapter: Boxo = enterprise risk removal; Airwallex = quota and cadence; Zhao = messy workflow scoping; Sunder = review-first document intelligence; Salescraft = AI-native GTM systems.

Use the Second Brain methodology layer:

- High-signal enterprise sales: finite territory, proof proximity, champion enablement, buyer-defined criteria, and no generic touches.
- AI-native account intelligence: stakeholder maps, signal evidence, deal-health fields, proof gaps, and next-best action queues.
- Agentic GTM campaign workflows: use AI to create evidence-backed recommendations and artifacts with human review, not noisy automation.
- Founder-led enterprise sales playbooks: discovery and demos should prove the buyer's use case, enterprise deals need milestone discipline, and the seller should help the champion safely carry the internal case.
- Buyer co-authorship: complex deals move when the buyer and seller co-author the internal story, proof criteria, implementation path, and risk answers, not when the vendor only forwards polished collateral.

## Primary Deal Card: Boxo / Unabank Enterprise Risk Removal

### Use This For

- "Walk me through a deal end to end."
- "How do you multi-thread?"
- "Tell me about a complex enterprise sale."
- "How did you navigate technical or compliance objections?"
- "What did you learn from a hard deal?"

### One-Line Thesis

> At Boxo, I learned enterprise sales the hard way: selling an early technical platform to a serious fintech buyer by making the deal underwriteable across executive value, product fit, engineering feasibility, and legal/compliance risk.

### Deal Snapshot

| Field | Draft answer |
|---|---|
| Company | Boxo / Appboxo |
| Buyer | Unabank-style fintech buyer. Exact public entity naming needs confirmation. |
| Product | SDK-based miniapp infrastructure for embedding third-party services inside a host mobile app. |
| Initial use case | Ecommerce marketplace inside a fintech app, with expansion into additional embedded service categories. |
| Commercial model | Remembered as base annual license around USD 150K-200K ARR, plus per-merchant expansion and custom implementation fees; broader remembered opportunity around USD 300K/year. |
| Result | First enterprise proof point, marketplace launched, eSIMs/travel went live, contributed to Boxo's USD 450K ARR and later enterprise credibility. |
| Seth role | Account orchestration, stakeholder mapping, product/commercial translation, pricing and legal/commercial negotiation. |

### Stakeholder Map

| Stakeholder | Likely priority | Seth's move |
|---|---|---|
| CEO in Singapore | Strategic upside, faster launch, engagement, monetization, investor/customer proof. | Kept conversation tied to business value and platform expansion. |
| Product Head in the Philippines | UX quality, product roadmap fit, reusable marketplace capability. | Translated the concept into product experience, rollout scope, and category expansion. |
| Engineering team in Ukraine | SDK/API feasibility, auth/payment/session flows, support ownership, build-vs-buy. | Slowed the deal down enough to answer real technical questions through Boxo's product team. |
| Legal/compliance/risk | Vendor maturity, data flow, consent, security posture, SLAs, liability, brand risk. | Made risk explicit and tied it to implementation facts rather than hand-waving. |
| Boxo product/internal team | What can actually be delivered, scoped, supported, and documented. | Brought buyer concerns back internally and avoided overpromising. |

### Value Driver

Unabank wanted to become more than a core financial app. The marketplace created more user engagement, more service categories, potential commerce/BNPL or lending tie-ins, faster launch cycles, and new monetization routes. The value was not "buy our SDK." It was: launch multiple embedded services faster than building every integration from scratch.

### Consultative Sales Motion

1. Qualified the strategic pain: expanding the app's service surface and launching faster.
2. Mapped the distributed buying committee across executive, product, engineering, and risk stakeholders.
3. Separated each stakeholder's concern: business upside, UX, technical feasibility, compliance, support, and vendor maturity.
4. Converted objections into scoping questions for the internal product team.
5. Used in-person trust-building where needed because Boxo lacked mature enterprise proof.
6. Expanded the deal from a single ecommerce integration into a reusable embedded-services platform.
7. Kept the close grounded in what Boxo could actually deliver.

### Buyer Co-Authorship And Champion Safety

Use this when an interviewer asks what made the deal consultative rather than just persistent.

| Sales problem | Seth's move | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|
| Young-vendor risk | Made scope, support, security, data flow, SLAs, and escalation explicit. | Gave the buyer a way to defend the risk internally. |
| Distributed stakeholders | Split the account into CEO, product, engineering, legal/compliance, and internal product threads. | Avoided treating one enthusiastic stakeholder as full deal consensus. |
| Build-vs-buy skepticism | Reframed Boxo as faster embedded-services launch capacity and partner-network leverage. | Moved the conversation from feature parity to business speed and expansion. |
| Champion enablement | Helped convert objections into concrete proof and implementation answers. | The champion needed internal confidence, not just more vendor enthusiasm. |
| Proof gap | Stayed honest about maturity and brought hard questions back to product. | Credibility came from precision and follow-through, not pretending Boxo was already enterprise-perfect. |

**Strong interview line:**

> The deal moved when it became co-authored. I was not just trying to convince them from the outside; I was helping the buyer build the internal case: why this workflow, why this vendor risk was manageable, what technical proof mattered, and what expansion path justified the effort.

### Objections And Responses

|---|---|---|
| "We can build this ourselves." | Product Head, engineering | Position Boxo as speed plus partner-network leverage, not just code. |
| "Will this feel native enough?" | Product Head | Clarify UX, miniapp experience, implementation scope, and reusable launch pattern. |
| "Can we trust a young vendor?" | CEO, engineering, legal/compliance | Be clear on scope, support, SLAs, security posture, and ownership. |

### Three-Minute Walkthrough

> The best enterprise deal example is Boxo / Unabank. Boxo was early then, and we were selling SDK-based miniapp infrastructure to fintech and super-app buyers. The product let a host app embed third-party services inside its own app experience, so the buyer could launch new service categories faster without building every integration from scratch.
>
> The initial Unabank use case was an ecommerce marketplace inside their fintech app. At the executive level, the business case was strong: more user engagement, more monetization routes, potential commerce and lending tie-ins, and faster time-to-market. But because they were a fintech and we were a young startup, the risk was real.
>
> The stakeholder map was distributed. I was dealing directly with the CEO in Singapore and the Product Head in the Philippines, while working through engineering feasibility with their team in Ukraine. Legal and compliance were involved around the normal fintech-vendor questions: data flow, security posture, support, SLAs, user consent, and liability.
>
> My role was to make the deal underwriteable. I mapped who cared about what, kept the CEO conversation tied to strategic value, worked with the Product Head on whether it could become a real product experience, brought engineering's concerns back to our product team, and personally led pricing and legal/commercial negotiation because we were a small team.
>
> The hardest moment was engineering skepticism. They had a fair question: why trust a young startup's SDK when they could build internally? I could not just say "trust us." I had to slow the deal down, clarify SDK/API feasibility, auth and payment flows, support ownership, and what would actually be scoped.
>
> The commercial move was to avoid pricing it as one marketplace integration. I reframed it as a broader embedded-services layer: if this works for ecommerce, it can become the way they launch more categories like eSIMs and travel. That created a land-and-expand model.
>
> The result was our first enterprise proof point, remembered around the low-six-figure annual range, and it contributed to Boxo's broader USD 450K ARR across fintech clients. More importantly, it taught me that enterprise sales is structured risk removal. The seller's job is to find every reason the buyer cannot say yes yet, then make those risks concrete enough to resolve.

### 60-Second Version

> At Boxo, the best example is Unabank. We were early and selling miniapp infrastructure to a serious fintech buyer, so the deal was not just a commercial pitch. The CEO cared about strategic upside: launching an ecommerce marketplace and more embedded services faster. The Product Head cared about whether it would become a real product experience. Engineering cared about SDK/API feasibility, auth and payment flows, and support ownership. Legal/compliance cared about vendor risk, data flow, SLAs, and liability.
>
> My role was to hold those threads together: map stakeholders, translate objections back to product, keep the executive case tied to business value, and lead pricing and legal/commercial negotiation. The result was a first enterprise proof point and the lesson that enterprise sales is risk removal, not just persuasion.

### Follow-Up Answers

**What did you personally do?**

> I was the commercial orchestrator. I qualified the account, mapped the stakeholders, ran the business-value narrative, worked with product on feasibility answers, handled pricing and legal/commercial negotiation, and expanded the deal from one use case into a broader platform conversation.

**What was the hardest part?**

> The maturity gap. They were a serious fintech buyer and we were still an early startup. The buyer had every reason to ask hard questions about technical feasibility, support, and vendor risk. I had to create confidence without pretending we were more mature than we were.

**How did you multi-thread?**

> I separated the account by stakeholder priority. CEO: strategic upside and speed. Product: UX and roadmap fit. Engineering: integration feasibility. Legal/compliance: data, security, support, and liability. Internally: what Boxo could actually deliver. Each thread needed different proof.

**What did you learn?**

> The best stakeholder objections are not annoyances. They show you the real risk you need to remove. If engineering says no, that may be the most useful signal in the deal because they are telling you what has to become true before the buyer can safely proceed.

**How did you enable the champion?**

> I tried to make the internal case easier to carry. That meant giving the business sponsor a clear value story, giving product a concrete user-experience path, giving engineering real implementation answers, and giving legal/compliance a contained risk picture. In enterprise sales, the champion is often taking career risk by advocating for a young vendor, so the seller's job is to make that advocacy safer.

**How does this map to OpenAI?**

> OpenAI enterprise adoption will also involve executive appetite, technical experimentation, product/workflow fit, and governance concerns. The BDR value is not just creating interest; it is helping the Account Director understand which workflow, stakeholder map, proof gaps, and risk questions make the opportunity real.

### Value Driver

The value was finance workflow improvement: lower FX and payment friction, faster overseas supplier payments, better account/card/spend visibility, cleaner reconciliation, and less manual finance work. The sales motion worked best when the outreach and discovery started with how money moved through the business.

### Sales Process

1. Market map Singapore segments with cross-border money movement.
2. Prioritize ICPs with real FX, supplier-payment, reconciliation, or card/spend pain.
3. Research the business model before outreach.
4. Lead with workflow pain, not generic fintech features.
5. Qualify currencies, payment frequency, approximate volume, current providers, pain, urgency, and buyer owner.
6. Map the right Airwallex bundle to the actual workflow.
7. Hand over clean context to AEs or drive the next step directly.
8. Use closed-won/lost learning to tighten ICP, messaging, and handoffs.

#### One-Line Thesis

#### Snapshot

#### 60-Second Answer

> Zhao / Leader Food is my cleanest messy-ops AI story. The customer had orders coming in through WhatsApp and WeChat groups, but the hard part was not just reading messages. Customers used shorthand, Chinese, broken Chinese, voice notes, images, vague product names, and customer-specific ordering habits. Staff had to translate that into the right SKU/spec before the morning warehouse, driver, and ERP workflow.
>
> I scoped it as a review-first OrderOps layer, not an ERP replacement. The system captured messages, preserved raw evidence, matched against SKU data, customer history, aliases, and route context, flagged ambiguity for human review, generated the morning picklist/Bible and driver route stack, and produced ERP/import-ready outputs while leaving ERP as the source of truth.
>
> Commercially, I packaged it as a S$40K fixed-scope project with deposit, MVP, final acceptance, UAT, support, assumptions, and explicit exclusions. The lesson is directly relevant to enterprise AI sales: the hard work is understanding where the workflow starts, where truth lives, what needs human approval, and what artifact the business actually runs on.

#### OpenAI Relevance

This is the story for "workflow qualification." It shows why an enterprise AI opportunity cannot be qualified by asking whether a customer is "interested in AI." You have to know the source systems, handoff points, review rules, approval thresholds, exception paths, and operational metric.

#### 60-Second Answer

> Sunder is my strongest document-processing story. We ran a paid pilot with Hoh Law, a leading Singapore personal-injury and road-accident claims firm. The pain was not that lawyers did not know the law. The pain was that high-volume PIPD creates messy evidence packets: bills, reports, income documents, scans, photos, duplicates, and combined PDFs. Someone has to turn that into a file a lawyer can actually review.
>
> We built it as a review-first workflow, not a legal chatbot. Files went into a case workspace. Gemini handled classification and splitting. Extend AI handled structured extraction with citations. Hoh-specific schemas captured medical expenses, medical reports, income evidence, payer categories, dates, providers, and suspicious or missing fields. Reviewers could inspect the value against the source PDF, correct it, and generate a claimant-side review pack or first-draft letter-of-demand support.
>
> The important commercial lesson was scoping. We deliberately focused on evidence prep, citations, validation, and human review, not replacing legal judgment. That is how I think serious enterprise AI needs to be sold: narrow enough to trust, useful enough to matter, and connected to the real workflow.

#### OpenAI Relevance

This is the story for "trust-heavy document intelligence." It maps cleanly to OpenAI enterprise selling because the buyer is not only buying model capability. They are buying source grounding, review surfaces, evals, exception handling, auditability, and a workflow that legal, security, operations, and leadership can trust.
