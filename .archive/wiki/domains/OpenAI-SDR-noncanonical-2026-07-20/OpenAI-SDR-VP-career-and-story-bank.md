## Core Career Narrative

### 30-Second Version

> My background is enterprise GTM first, AI systems second, and the throughline is turning messy workflows into structured opportunities. At Boxo, I learned enterprise sales in ambiguity by selling a technical platform to fintech and super-app buyers. At Airwallex, I proved the same muscles in a mature, quota-driven fintech motion. Then Salescraft and GTM Workspace were the AI-native extension of that work: account intelligence, source-backed research, CRM hygiene, outbound, and seller handoffs. The reason OpenAI fits is that this Strategic BDR role sits exactly at that intersection: enterprise AI demand, account strategy, product signal, stakeholder mapping, and high-quality opportunity creation.

### 2-Minute Version

> The simple arc is that I started in law, but moved closer and closer to how companies actually get built: first through enterprise GTM, then through AI systems.
>
> I studied law at Cambridge, and the first real technology signal for me was my dissertation on AI use in sentencing. I would not claim I became an ML engineer there, but it gave me the systems lens: institutions, decisions, evidence, human judgment, and where technology could become leverage.
>
> Boxo was my operator apprenticeship. I joined early and had to sell before the playbook really existed. The product was a technical miniapp platform, and the strongest lesson was that enterprise sales is not just persuasion. It is structured risk removal: mapping the CEO, product, engineering, legal, and compliance concerns, and making the deal safe enough for a serious buyer.
>
> Airwallex was the quota and cadence chapter. I joined as the second outbound hire for Singapore, built pipeline in a competitive fintech market, hit over 200% quota, closed 22 logos, and coached junior SDRs. That gave me a more disciplined operating system for ICP, research, follow-up, handoffs, and pipeline review.
>
> Salescraft was the natural next step. I had lived the manual GTM loop, so I started building AI systems around it: TAM research, enrichment, scoring, signal-based outbound, CRM hygiene, and seller handoffs. Then GTM Workspace and later Sunder/Neo Bot pushed that into broader AI workflow systems with source boundaries, review gates, automations, and verification.
>
> So the reason OpenAI is interesting is not just brand or title. It is the chance to do enterprise AI GTM at the frontier, close to product signal and Account Director strategy, while bringing my mix of regulated-buyer experience, stakeholder mapping, and AI-native workflow thinking.

## Role Cards

### Boxo Role Card

**Mandate:** Create enterprise and platform partnership motion before the company had mature enterprise proof.

**What Seth did:**

- Sold SDK-based miniapp infrastructure to fintech and super-app buyers.
- Directly sourced and closed USD 450K ARR across 3 fintech clients.
- Led enterprise sales through discovery, RFPs, legal/commercial negotiation, technical scoping, procurement risk, and integration concerns.
- Closed 9 strategic super-app partnerships including Binance, GCash, and Touch 'n Go.

**What worked:**

- Mapping stakeholders by risk: CEO, product, engineering, legal/compliance, partner teams.
- Framing Boxo as faster embedded-services launch capacity, not only an SDK.
- Turning one use case into platform expansion.

**What did not work or was hard:**

- Young-vendor credibility was a real constraint.
- Technical and compliance questions could not be hand-waved.
- The enterprise motion needed to be built while selling.

**What Seth learned:**

> Enterprise sales is structured risk removal. The seller's job is to find every reason the buyer cannot safely say yes yet, then make those risks concrete enough to resolve.

**Why move on:**

> Boxo taught me ambiguity and enterprise risk removal. I wanted to test that in a more mature, quota-driven fintech environment with a sharper operating cadence.

### Airwallex Role Card

**Mandate:** Build Singapore outbound motion in a mature, competitive fintech environment.

**What Seth did:**

- Joined as second outbound hire for Singapore.
- Built ICP, messaging tests, account lists, and pipeline into mid-market accounts.
- Sold finance workflow pain across payments, FX, transfers, cards, and spend-related workflows.
- Achieved 200%+ annual quota attainment and closed 22 new logos.

**What worked:**

- Segmenting around cross-border money movement and finance operations pain.

**What did not work or was hard:**

**What Seth learned:**

> GTM performance is an operating system: ICP, research, qualification, follow-up, handoffs, CRM hygiene, coaching, and pipeline review.

**Why move on:**

> AI became the obvious next curve, and I had lived the exact manual GTM loop that AI could improve. Salescraft was a way to turn that operating pain into systems.

### Salescraft Role Card

**Mandate:** Build AI-assisted GTM systems for startups and convert manual sales research into repeatable workflows.

**What Seth did:**

- Built AI GTM systems for 8 tech startups.

**What Seth learned:**

> AI should scale seller judgment, not replace it. The useful system has source evidence, routing state, confidence labels, approved messaging lanes, and human review.

**Why move on:**

> Salescraft showed me the value of AI in GTM, but it also made me want deeper builder fluency: workflows, APIs, state, review surfaces, and verification.

### GTM Workspace Role Card

**Mandate:** Build a company second brain and AI-assisted GTM operating workspace.

**What Seth did:**

- Built an operating workspace for Acme GTM work in about a month.
- Kept Drive as official document truth, repo/QMD as searchable memory, Notion as current state, and agents as reviewed workers.
- Built workflows for prebriefs, postbriefs/pulse, pricing, SOW drafting, meeting memory, account/deal research, and agent command center review.
- Added source boundaries, review flags, automations, and tests.

**What worked:**

- Separating official truth, current state, durable memory, and generated drafts.
- Human review before external send, publishing, pricing, legal, or CRM-impacting actions.

**What did not work or was hard:**

- Search alone was not enough; freshness and source ownership mattered.
- Agents needed live operating state, not only long-term memory.
- Recurring automations needed boundaries and a doctor check.

**What Seth learned:**

> A company brain needs source boundaries before it needs intelligence. AI is useful only when the system knows what it is allowed to read, write, stage, and never do.

**Why it matters for OpenAI:**

> This is directly relevant to enterprise AI adoption: customers do not just need models. They need source-of-truth design, governance, review workflows, and a path from pilot to operating system.

**Mandate:** Turn messy workflows into review-first AI products.

**What Seth did:**

- Built Sunder workspace for legal document workflows: classification, splitting, extraction, citations, validation, review, and report generation.

**What worked:**

- Workflow-before-model.
- Schemas and validation as product decisions.
- Human review as a first-class surface.
- Evals, traces, browser checks, and acceptance criteria.

**What did not work or was hard:**

- The hardest workflow logic lived in operators' heads.
- AI extraction failed on ambiguity, missing evidence, stale source data, and edge cases.
- Agents needed scoped tools, approvals, and verification to be useful.

**What Seth learned:**

> The best AI systems are not magic. They are workflow products: source of truth, state machine, schema, review UI, evaluation loop, and trust boundary.

## Playbook Answer

### Prompt

"What playbook did you build?"

### Best Answer

> The most relevant playbook is my AI-native account-intelligence and enterprise outbound playbook. It came from Airwallex and Salescraft. The premise is that in strategic accounts, every low-context touch spends trust, so the job is to find the few accounts and people where timing, workflow pain, and proof are strongest.
>
> The playbook has six parts: define the account universe, capture source-backed signals, decide whether the signal is actually messageable, map stakeholders, identify proof gaps, and route the next best action to the seller. The output should be the top few people worth contacting today and the reason now, not just a bigger send list. AI helps with research, enrichment, summarization, draft angles, and CRM hygiene, but humans own the commercial judgment and final outreach.
>
> The reason it matters for OpenAI is that Strategic BDR work should not be generic volume. It should create account theses an Account Director can actually use.

### 60-Second Answer

> There are two categories. On the sales side, I helped build outbound and account-intelligence systems: ICP definition, account lists, signal capture, enrichment, scoring, messaging tests, CRM hygiene, and seller handoffs. That shows up at Airwallex and then more explicitly in Salescraft.
>

The sales version is "which account, which person, why now, what proof is missing, what should happen next?" The AI product version is the same discipline applied to workflow state and trust boundaries.

## Lost Or Stalled Deal Answer

Use this if asked before Seth confirms a specific lost logo.

> I do not want to invent a lost logo from memory, so the most honest example I would use is a GTM miss from Salescraft. Early on, it was tempting to treat AI prospecting as a copywriting problem: better personalization, more messages, more automation. What I learned is that if the list, signal, offer, or timing is wrong, AI just makes the wrong motion faster.
>
> The miss was not one dramatic account loss. It was campaign learning: some signals looked like intent but did not change seller action, some databases looked complete but missed local-market reality, and some AI-written messaging sounded personalized but did not carry a strong commercial premise.
>
> What changed afterward was my operating model. I started separating signal from scoring, requiring source URLs and confidence labels, routing weak signals to nurture instead of outbound, and diagnosing whether a miss came from list, offer, copy, deliverability, or timing before changing the message.

If the interviewer demands a traditional lost-deal story, use this boundary:

> I can talk through a stalled/hard deal in detail, but I would rather not manufacture a named lost deal without checking the exact details. The behavioral lesson is the same: when something does not move, I diagnose the failure layer before trying to sell harder.
