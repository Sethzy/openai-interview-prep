You have to understand where the use case sits in the business, who owns the workflow, what system is the source of truth, what risk would block adoption, and what proof would make the buyer comfortable moving from curiosity to a real pilot.

## How Do You Use Codex Daily As Your Main Driver?

### Core Answer

> The best way to describe it is that Codex has become the place where my work can live, not just a coding assistant.
>
> The big idea I took from the Codex-maxxing article is that the unit of work is no longer one prompt and one answer. It is an operating loop: a durable thread, shared memory, tools, steering, recurring checks, clear goals, and an artifact surface where I can review the work.
>
> Durable, auto-compacting threads matter because they let me turn Codex into something closer to a personal chief of staff. The important part is not just that the context window is large. It is that a pinned thread can keep compressing and preserving the important decisions, preferences, tasks, and open loops over time. Instead of starting from zero every day, I can keep one operating thread that works from a canonical markdown task list, knows the running context, and keeps a tracker of open loops.
>
> The shape is: here is my plan, here are the tasks, here are the accounts or people I am working on, and here are the rules for how I want to operate. On a one-hour heartbeat, check the places where work actually appears: Gmail, Calendar, Slack, WhatsApp where available, and any Sheets or trackers. Then surface only what matters, flag conflicts, propose what I should do next, and draft the first-pass replies, follow-ups, meeting notes, outreach updates, or research summaries. I still use my own judgment before sending anything, but the expensive part of staying on top of context is already done.
>
> Voice input gets more of my actual thinking into Codex.
>
> The benefit is not speed. It is that the agent gets the unedited version of my thinking. Codex has built-in voice input, but I also use Wispr Flow because system-wide dictation changes how much context I can feed into everything else too. If I am planning a piece of work, I might say, "I think there is some guy named Ben in Slack who mentioned this, I do not remember exactly what, just go look." That sentence is too vague and annoying to type, but completely natural to say.
>
> The same thing applies to transcripts. If I want to write a post, I can call someone, record the conversation, or talk to them in person with Granola on my phone, then use the transcript as the starting material. A lot of plans get better when the model has access to the messy version of what I think, not just the polished one.
>
> Voice becomes more useful when combined with steering.
>
> Steering means adding more direction while Codex is already working instead of waiting for the current step to finish. It lets you inject the next message after a tool call. If I am reviewing a website, I can keep talking while I look at it: make this smaller, this copy is wrong, the spacing between these two things feels off, once this is done, open a PR, wait for the preview deploy, send the preview link to the person who needs to review it on Slack.
>
> I do not need to wait for each step to finish before deciding the next one. I can keep adding intent while the agent is still working, then walk away with the queue already shaped. Later, Heartbeats can monitor the PR or Slack thread after I leave. The unit of work stops being "one prompt, one answer." It becomes a small operating loop.
>
> Once threads started lasting longer, they needed shared memory outside any one repo.
>
> The important move is saving things to disk. A long thread can remember a lot, but that memory is trapped inside the thread unless the useful parts get written somewhere durable. The point of the memory system is to turn what the thread learns into artifacts I can inspect, edit, diff, and reuse.
>
> Most of my long-running threads start in an Obsidian vault:
>
> ```text
> vault/
> ├── TODO.md
> ├── people/
> ├── projects/
> ├── agent/
> └── notes/
> ```
>
> At the top level, I keep `AGENTS.md` instructions that tell the model to write things down: as you learn more about people, make progress on projects, make a decision, or close an open loop, update the relevant pages in the vault.
>
> The vault is where the agent lives, separate from any one project. Repositories hold code. The vault holds rolling context around my work: people, decisions, open loops, daily notes, project state, and the bits of understanding that would otherwise get lost between threads.
>
> Once a thread has memory, the next question is what it can touch. The useful distinction in my head is: `$browser` is for local web surfaces I want to inspect and annotate; `@chrome` is for signed-in browser state and multiple tabs; `@computer` is for work that only exists as a GUI.
>
> If I am iterating on a local app, I want `$browser`. If I need to work inside a logged-in browser session, I want `@chrome`. If the only way to do the task is to click through a desktop app or portal, I want `@computer`.
>
> That matters a lot for life admin and back-office work because many real workflows do not have clean APIs. Taxes, government portals, booking links, vendor forms, supplier research, expense flows, insurance forms, or account settings often just live behind a website or desktop interface. I still review and approve the output, but Codex can gather context, click through the boring parts, prepare the draft, and leave me with the decision.
>
> Connectors like Gmail, Calendar, Slack, Sheets, and messaging surfaces are useful because a lot of work starts in inboxes, calendars, and conversations before it becomes a clean task. Heartbeats and automations make recurring loops possible: check the inbox, prep the task list before I wake up, keep tasks clean without duplicate entries, watch the calendar for conflicts, schedule meetings from booking links, update an outreach tracker or CRM from email activity, research a supplier or partner, or draft the next follow-up if someone has not replied.
>
> Remote control makes these longer loops feel portable. OpenAI describes Codex mobile as a way to work with Codex from anywhere, and the practical value is that Codex can keep working from the machine where my files, permissions, credentials, and local setup already live while I check in from my phone.
>
> That changes the rhythm of work. I can kick off a task, go for a walk or do groceries, and still review what Codex found, answer a question, approve the next step, or change direction from mobile. The work does not have to pause just because I am not at my desk. A thread can keep going, and I can give just enough attention to unblock the next move.
>
> Goals are how I try to keep longer tasks honest. A weak goal is just "implement this plan." A strong goal has a success criterion: pass these tests, produce this artifact, verify this output, or stop when this condition is met. That keeps the agent pointed at an outcome rather than just generating motion.
>
> The side panel is probably the biggest shift in how it feels. Codex can create or inspect markdown, spreadsheets, PDFs, slides, HTML pages, local apps, or browser surfaces, and I can look at the same object, annotate it, and review changes. That turns the output into a work product, not just a chat response.
>
> The learning piece is becoming more concrete for me too. When I finish a Codex session and there is a real artifact or diff, I can ask Codex to teach me from the work it just did. I have an `explain-diff-html` skill that turns a code change into a self-contained HTML lesson: background on the existing system, the intuition behind the change, a walkthrough of the code, diagrams or toy examples, and a short quiz.
>
> That is different from just asking for a summary. If an agent changed a part of the codebase I do not fully understand, the goal is to build my mental model back up to the level where I can review, modify, and explain the work. Geoffrey Litt had a line I liked about agents writing code that helps humans understand code. That is exactly the pattern: the agent does not just produce the output; it can also produce the review surface that helps me judge the output.
>
> The key idea is that the artifact becomes the teaching surface. Instead of rubber-stamping a diff, I can ask Codex: explain the existing system, show me why this change works, walk through the important code paths, call out the failure modes, and test me on it. The HTML format matters because diagrams, annotated snippets, examples, and quiz questions make the explanation much easier to actually review than a wall of markdown.
>
> That captures how I want to use AI generally: not to outsource thinking, but to compress the loop between doing, feedback, and understanding.
>
> So when I say I use Codex daily, I do not mean "I ask it to write things." I mean I use it to keep work moving through persistent context, tools, verification, and review. That is also why OpenAI feels specific to me. I have already changed my own operating cadence around these tools, and I want to help companies find the places where the same kind of shift is real for them.

### Short Version

> Codex is my main workbench. The important shift is that work no longer starts with a blank page or ends in a chat transcript. A good Codex loop can act like a personal chief of staff: one durable thread, one canonical markdown task list, memory written into files, tools that can inspect inboxes, calendars, booking links, and trackers, heartbeats for recurring checks, goals with verification, and a side panel where I can review the artifact or draft before acting. That is the pattern I think enterprises will move toward too: not AI as a writing shortcut, but AI as an operating loop around real work.

### Proof Points To Pull In

- **Personal chief of staff thread:** a large-context, auto-compacting pinned thread can run from one canonical markdown task list, maintain a tracker, and preserve preferences, decisions, open loops, and context.
- **Voice and transcripts:** the benefit is not speed; it is getting the unedited version of Seth's thinking into Codex.
- **Steering:** Seth can add direction while Codex is already working, so the unit becomes a queue or loop rather than one prompt.
- **File-backed memory:** useful context gets written into an Obsidian-style vault with TODOs, people, projects, agent notes, daily notes, decisions, and open loops.
- **Computer and browser use:** `$browser` is for local web surfaces, `@chrome` is for signed-in browser state, and `@computer` is for work that only exists in a GUI.
- **Life admin / no-API workflows:** taxes, portals, booking links, vendor forms, expense flows, insurance forms, and account settings often live behind websites or desktop interfaces.
- **Connectors:** Gmail, Calendar, Slack, Sheets, booking links, and messaging surfaces let Codex work where the task actually starts.
- **Heartbeats and automations:** recurring checks can monitor inboxes, calendar conflicts, follow-ups, duplicate tasks, trackers, suppliers, partners, and changing conditions.
- **Mobile / remote control:** Seth can start a longer task, step away from the desk, then review, approve, answer, or redirect from mobile while Codex keeps working from the machine with the right files and permissions.
- **Goals:** long tasks need success criteria and verification, not just a Markdown plan.
- **Side panel:** artifacts can be inspected, annotated, and revised in the same loop as the agent's work.
- **Post-session teaching loop:** after a coding session, Seth can use `explain-diff-html` to turn the actual diff into an HTML lesson with system background, intuition, walkthrough, diagrams, examples, and a quiz.
- **Geoffrey Litt frame:** agents can write artifacts that help humans understand code, so the review surface becomes part of the work.
- **HTML diff explainers:** diagrams, annotated snippets, toy examples, and quiz questions make the output easier to judge than a plain summary.

### Avoid

- Do not make it sound like Codex replaces Seth's judgment.
