## V. Discovery, Qualification And Deal Advancement

### 54. When do you disqualify or slow down a deal?

> I slow a deal down when an important qualification condition is still unknown. I disqualify it when the evidence shows that condition is unlikely to become true.
>
> I look at five foundations: problem, priority, people, process and proof. Is there a meaningful problem we can solve? Is it important enough to act on now? Is there an accountable owner and a path to the wider buying group? Is there a credible decision and implementation process? And will the buyer commit to a proof step tied to a measurable outcome?
>
> If one of those is missing but discoverable, I name the gap, agree on what would resolve it and slow my investment until that evidence appears. If the account has no real priority, will not provide access, requires something we cannot responsibly support, or wants open-ended custom work without commercial commitment, I would disqualify it or move it into nurture.
>
> At UNA, for example, I would have slowed or disqualified the opportunity if the eSIM initiative had been merely exploratory, if the Head of Partnerships could not widen access, if Product or Engineering rejected the architecture, or if the risk process exposed an unsupported requirement. Instead, UNA demonstrated a live initiative, an accountable champion, technical engagement, a lightweight RFP and a path to CEO approval.
>
> The principle is simple: I do not keep a “maybe” deal alive to protect pipeline optics. I protect selling time and the Account Director's attention by being explicit about what is missing and what would make us walk away.

**Disposition framework:**

| Evidence state | Seller response |
|---|---|
| Foundation confirmed | Advance against the next buyer milestone |
| Foundation unknown but testable | Slow down and run a specific validation step |
| Timing absent but structural fit remains | Nurture around a defined trigger |
| Structural fit, access or responsible deployment path is absent | Disqualify or close-lost |

**The five foundations:**

1. **Problem:** A consequential workflow or business gap exists.
2. **Priority:** The buyer has a credible reason to address it now.
3. **People:** There is an owner, potential champion and path to decision authority.
4. **Process:** The decision, risk review, implementation and paper path are credible.
5. **Proof:** The buyer will help define and support evidence that could justify a decision.

**Raw-source basis:** C1 provides the Seth-confirmed UNA disqualification conditions. C2 supplies the disciplined-no answer and warns against enthusiasm without ownership, access or deployment readiness. S3 lines 81–100 identifies “maybe” deals, open-ended trials and pre-contract custom work as qualification risks. S1 lines 125–148 and S4 lines 181–205 support buyer-co-authored evidence, concrete next steps and explicit deal-health red flags. The five-part structure is an interview synthesis, not a claimed historical Boxo or Airwallex methodology.

### 55. How do you know a deal has actually advanced?

> I know a deal has advanced when the buyer's decision state changes—not when I complete an activity or move the CRM stage.
>
> I look for five kinds of evidence. The buyer has corrected or validated the problem and business case. We have gained access to a stakeholder required for the decision. The buyer has committed people, data, time or volume to a proof step. A technical, commercial or risk blocker has been resolved. Or there is a dated internal decision event with a clear owner and purpose.
>
> A demo, proposal or friendly follow-up is not progress by itself. Those activities count only when they produce new buyer evidence.
>
> UNA is a good example. The opportunity advanced when the Head of Partnerships engaged around a real eSIM initiative; again when Product and Engineering validated a supportable approach; again when the lightweight RFP and legal/compliance review progressed; and again when the CEO approved the commercial case. Each stage removed a different reason the customer could not yet buy.
>
> My deal review would therefore ask: what can the buyer do, decide or defend today that they could not do before the last interaction? If the answer is nothing, the deal probably has not advanced.

**Advancement evidence:**

1. **Validated:** The buyer corrects or approves the problem, value or success criteria.
2. **Accessed:** A required stakeholder becomes meaningfully engaged.
3. **Committed:** The buyer contributes resources to a proof or decision step.
4. **De-risked:** A named technical, security, legal, commercial or implementation blocker is resolved.
5. **Scheduled:** A buyer-owned internal decision event or dated mutual next step exists.

**Seller activity versus buyer advancement:**

| Seller activity | Evidence that would make it advancement |
|---|---|
| Demo delivered | Buyer-defined criteria were tested and a result was accepted |
| Proposal sent | Buyer corrected the scope, value case or commercial terms |
| Stakeholder meeting held | The stakeholder validated their decision criterion or accepted an action |
| Security material shared | A named risk owner reviewed it and closed or precisely defined the gap |
| Follow-up booked | The meeting has a decision purpose, required attendees and exit criterion |

**Raw-source basis:** C1 provides the confirmed UNA stage sequence. C2 and the original sales-acumen pack state that advancement requires buyer correction, stakeholder access, agreed proof, a named internal meeting, a resolved blocker or a dated next step. S4 lines 100–125 supports milestone-based probability and buyer edits, redlines, internal meetings and next-step urgency as stronger evidence than activity. S1 lines 104–138 supports buyer-side co-authorship and customer-verifiable exit criteria. MEDDICC language is not required for this answer.

### 56. How do you identify a compelling event or genuine “why now”?

> I treat a compelling event as a business event with a consequence, an accountable owner and a time boundary. A public announcement or executive comment may be a useful signal, but it is not yet urgency.
>
> I test four things. What is changing? What must be true by when? What happens if the buyer misses it? And whose objective, risk or credibility is attached to the outcome?
>
> I then validate the event in discovery rather than telling the buyer they should feel urgent. I ask what the date is connected to—a launch, customer commitment, seasonal peak, board mandate, contract renewal, risk deadline or cost threshold—and whether the organization has actually allocated attention or resources to it.
>
> UNA's eSIM expansion illustrates the distinction. The public signal told me where to investigate. It became a genuine “why now” only after we confirmed that eSIMs were an active launch initiative owned by the Head of Partnerships and connected to UNA's wider goal of launching additional merchant categories. Delaying the capability meant delaying that expansion and continuing to consume internal engineering capacity category by category.
>
> If there is no consequence, owner or credible time boundary, I treat the event as a research signal or nurture trigger—not as manufactured urgency.

**Compelling-event test:**

| Question | Evidence sought |
|---|---|
| What is changing? | A real business event or internal mandate |
| By when? | A credible date, sequence or time-bound operating window |
| What happens if it slips? | Revenue, cost, risk, customer, capacity or strategic consequence |
| Who owns it? | A person whose objective or credibility is affected |

**Useful discovery questions:**

- “What is driving the timing of this initiative?”
- “What has to be true before the launch or decision can happen?”
- “If this slips by a quarter, what changes operationally or commercially?”
- “Who inside the business is accountable for that outcome?”
- “What resources have already been committed?”

**Raw-source basis:** C1 establishes UNA's active eSIM initiative, Head of Partnerships ownership, build-versus-buy context and platform-expansion objective. C2 supports distinguishing messageable signals from real buyer urgency. S1 lines 127–148 and 154–160 supports tying “why now” to an executive priority, funding and the stakeholder with the most to gain. S4 lines 185–203 treats explicit internal pressure, deadline and budget path as positive deal evidence. No exact UNA launch date has been verified, so the answer does not invent one.

### 57. How do you uncover budget and the buying process?

> I uncover budget and process in parallel, after I understand enough value and scope to ask intelligently. “Do you have budget?” is too shallow because a buyer may have money but no approval path, or a strong priority but need to create or reallocate funding.
>
> On budget, I want to know the value category, funding source and authority. Is this expected to drive revenue, reduce cost, protect a mission-critical workflow or remain a nice-to-have? Is the spend already funded, does it need an offset, or is the status unknown? Which team owns the budget, who can reallocate it and what level of investment requires executive approval?
>
> On process, I ask how the company bought a comparable strategic product last time. Who evaluates the business case and technical fit? Who handles Security, Legal, Finance and Procurement? Is an RFP required? Who owns implementation? What are the approval thresholds, sequence, dependencies and target decision date?
>
> At UNA, we learned that the economic comparison was Boxo versus hiring and maintaining engineers internally. The account moved through Product and Engineering validation, a lightweight RFP, legal/compliance review and CEO approval. There was no material pricing objection. I can use that to show process discipline, but I would not invent UNA's exact budget bucket because it has not been verified.
>
> The output I want is a buyer-confirmed map: source of funds, economic buyer, decision criteria, approval and paper steps, implementation owner, risks, dates and the next unresolved decision.

**Budget map:**

1. **Value category:** Revenue, cost, risk or strategic capacity.
2. **Funding status:** Funded, requires reallocation or unknown.
3. **Funding source:** Which function or budget bucket would pay.
4. **Authority:** Who controls, reallocates and finally approves the investment.
5. **Commercial exposure:** Usage, support, implementation, tier or expansion implications the buyer must defend.

**Buying-process map:**

1. Business and technical decision criteria.
2. Champion, economic buyer and people who can say no.
3. Prior comparable purchase and actual approval sequence.
4. Security, privacy, legal, finance, procurement and RFP requirements.
5. Implementation owner, capacity and dependencies.
6. Paper-process owner, decision date and blocker owners.

**Speakable questions:**

> “How was a comparable investment approved last time?”
>
> “If the team agrees this is valuable, where would the funding come from and who can authorize it?”
>
> “Which technical, security, legal, finance or procurement reviews are required before the executive decision?”
>
> “Who owns implementation, and what capacity do they need to reserve?”

**Raw-source basis:** C1 provides UNA's verified build-versus-buy comparison, lightweight RFP, legal/compliance review and CEO decision authority. C2 warns that historical deal-specific budget mechanics are partly unknown. S1 lines 140–173 supports testing budget bucket, funding status, company-objective link, implementation effort and the economic-buyer path. S4 lines 127–159 provides finance-readiness and procurement fields. S3 lines 61–75 supports treating executive, security, procurement and legal stages as ordinary deal work rather than surprise blockers.

> UNA's precise budget source, approval threshold, Procurement role and internal sequence beyond the lightweight RFP and legal/compliance review are not verified. Do not invent them. MEDDICC can organize the explanation, but it was not confirmed as Boxo's historical methodology.

### 58. What does a high-quality next step look like?

> A high-quality next step is a mutual decision contract, not “let's catch up again.” It should name the purpose, people, preparation, owner, date and exit criterion.
>
> The purpose is the specific decision or proof gap the step must resolve. The people are the stakeholders required to resolve it. Preparation makes clear what each side will provide beforehand. Ownership assigns the actions. The date creates mutual commitment. And the exit criterion defines what we should know or agree afterward.
>
> For example, after confirming a payments problem at EU Holidays, a strong next step would not have been another general Airwallex presentation. It would have been a focused working session with the relevant Finance and operational stakeholders to validate payment volumes and corridors, agree the controlled FX scope and define what reliability evidence would justify expanding usage. I would send the draft scope; the buyer would confirm the relevant data and attendees; and we would leave with a go, no-go or precisely defined remaining gap.
>
> I try to schedule that step live and send a short written recap. If the buyer will not commit to the people, preparation or decision purpose, that is useful qualification evidence rather than something I hide behind a calendar invite.

**Next-step contract:**

1. **Purpose:** Which decision or missing proof will this step resolve?
2. **People:** Who must attend because they own that decision?
3. **Preparation:** What will the buyer and seller each provide beforehand?
4. **Owner:** Who is responsible for every action?
5. **Date:** When will the work and meeting happen?
6. **Exit criterion:** What observable outcome means the step succeeded?

**Weak versus strong:**

| Weak next step | Strong next step |
|---|---|
| “Send me a deck.” | Send a one-page case for the champion to correct before a named internal meeting |
| “Let's do another demo.” | Test buyer-defined criteria with the Product and technical evaluators |
| “Circle back next month.” | Reconnect after a named trigger, with owner and date |
| “Start a pilot.” | Run a scoped proof with workflow, metric, owner, duration and post-proof decision |

**Raw-source basis:** C1 confirms EU Holidays' measurable payment problems, Head of Finance champion and controlled FX proof path. The example is an explicitly structured illustration of a strong next step, not a claimed verbatim historical meeting agenda. C2 supplies the named-people, objective, owner, date and reason standard. S1 lines 104–138 supports concrete, near-term next steps and customer-verifiable exit criteria. S4 lines 110–125 and 195–203 supports live scheduling, named internal meetings, urgency and generic-collateral red flags.

## VI. Champions, Multi-Threading And Account Director Partnership

### 59. What makes a real champion?

> A real champion is someone who can move the deal when I am not in the room. I look for three things: incentive, access and action.
>
> Incentive means they have a business outcome or personal credibility attached to solving the problem. Access means they understand the organization and can help us navigate the people who must evaluate, approve, implement or block the decision. Action means they actually spend internal credibility: correcting our point of view, sharing difficult context, co-authoring the business case, arranging purposeful introductions and defending the decision internally.
>
> The Head of Finance at EU Holidays was a real champion because the payment workflow sat inside their remit, they helped us understand the wider decision and they supported a controlled path to action. Their value was not that they liked Airwallex. It was that they helped mobilize Finance, Operations, Commercial and the executive path around a problem they owned.
>
> My simplest test is: could this person explain why now, the business value, the principal risk and the next proof required to the rest of the company without me? If yes—and their behavior supports it—I have a champion. If they are enthusiastic but cannot or will not influence the internal process, I have a useful contact, not yet a champion.

**Champion standard:**

1. **Incentive:** Something meaningful to gain, protect or deliver.
2. **Access:** Knowledge of and credible paths through the buying organization.
3. **Action:** Observable internal work that advances the decision without the seller present.

**Raw-source basis:** C1 establishes the EU Holidays Head of Finance as the workflow owner and champion who helped navigate the buying group and supported controlled action. C2 supplies the “moves the deal when I am not in the room” distinction. S1 lines 140–177 distinguishes a real sales champion from an enthusiastic user and identifies incentive, process knowledge, CFO readiness and economic-buyer access. S4 lines 142–149 lists behavioral champion-risk fields.

### 60. How do you test whether someone is a champion or a friendly contact?

> I test champion quality through progressively meaningful behavior, not responsiveness.
>
> First, will they correct my account thesis and share the real consequence of doing nothing? Second, will they explain the internal decision process, likely objections and who else must believe? Third, will they co-author the proof criteria or business case in the customer's language? Fourth, will they create access to the right stakeholder for a specific reason? And finally, will they use the material when I am absent—by preparing an internal meeting, defending the case or returning with real feedback?
>
> Binance taught me why this matters. I had an engaged contact who asked detailed questions and spent time learning about Boxo. I interpreted that engagement as commitment. In reality, I did not reach or convince the economic buyer, force a clear build-versus-buy decision or establish that the contact would spend internal credibility. Binance chose to build internally.
>
> Since then, I distinguish learning behavior from mobilizing behavior. A friendly contact can be responsive and curious. A champion helps convert the conversation into an internal decision. Until I see that, I value the relationship but I do not overstate the opportunity.

**Five behavioral tests:**

| Test | Champion evidence |
|---|---|
| Correct the thesis | Shares consequences, incentives and what will not land internally |
| Reveal the process | Names the economic buyer, evaluators, blockers and approval path |
| Co-author the case | Edits proof criteria, value model or internal narrative |
| Create purposeful access | Introduces the stakeholder who owns the next decision risk |
| Mobilize internally | Uses the case without the seller and returns with real objections or commitments |

**Raw-source basis:** C2 records the Seth-confirmed Binance loss: the contact was engaged but behaved more like an information-gathering contact; Seth did not reach or convince the economic buyer; Binance chose internal build because it had the engineering resources. S1 lines 104–173 and S4 lines 142–149 support testing correction, insider context, stakeholder access, direct/private communication, internal defense and economic-buyer reach. C4 supports answering the literal question first and using one concrete proof point.

> [!warning] Truth boundary

### 61. How do you identify and reach the economic buyer?

> I identify the economic buyer by decision authority, not title alone. It is the person who owns the business-level outcome and can ultimately authorize the investment, accept the risk or choose the status quo.
>
> I triangulate that in discovery. Whose objective does this support? Which budget would fund it? Who can approve or reallocate that spend? Who would decide between buying, building and doing nothing? Who signs off when the business case, risk and commercial terms are complete?
>
> I normally reach that person through the champion, with a clear reason rather than a generic request for executive access. I align on what the economic buyer needs to decide, prepare a concise executive case covering why now, measurable value, major risks, implementation and the specific ask, and make the champion stronger in the meeting. If the champion cannot identify or create a credible path to that person, I treat it as a qualification risk.
>
> At EU Holidays, the Head of Finance was the champion and the CFO evaluated the financial-control and risk case, but the CEO was the final decision-maker. That is a useful distinction: an influential financial stakeholder is not automatically the economic buyer. My job was to connect the operational and commercial evidence to the level where the final business decision was made.
>
> I want economic-buyer alignment before the deal disappears into procurement, because forms can process a decision but they cannot create one.

**Economic-buyer test:**

1. Owns or is accountable for the business-level outcome.
2. Can authorize the investment or reallocate funding.
3. Can accept the strategic and operational risk.
4. Can choose the status quo, internal build or competing priority.
5. Has final decision authority after stakeholder evaluation.

**Executive-access request:**

> “We have validated the workflow and the controlled proof path. The remaining decision is whether the expected reliability and operational value justify shifting volume. Since the CEO owns that final business decision, would it help if we prepared a short executive review together covering the value, risks and specific approval required?”

**Raw-source basis:** C1 establishes the EU Holidays Head of Finance as champion, CFO as financial-control/risk stakeholder and CEO as final decision-maker. S1 lines 164–177 defines the economic buyer as the person with authority over the business decision and treats champion-provided access as a qualification signal. ### 62. How do you build a complete stakeholder map?

> I build a stakeholder map around the decisions that must be made, not around an org chart or a list of senior titles.
>
> I start with the active workflow and ask who owns the outcome, who experiences the problem, who can champion change, who validates product and technical fit, who controls security and governance, who approves the economics, who runs Legal or Procurement, who implements the solution and who can block it. The same person may hold more than one role, and some roles may not be required for every deal.
>
> For each stakeholder, I track six things: their decision role, desired outcome, concern or proof requirement, influence, current stance, and the next evidence or action needed. I also mark whether the person is inferred, identified, engaged or validated, because a name in the CRM is not yet a relationship.
>
> At UNA, the Head of Partnerships was the champion and initiative owner; the Product Head evaluated roadmap and customer experience; Engineering evaluated the SDK, authentication, payments, maintenance and implementation ownership; legal, compliance and risk evaluated data boundaries, liability, service levels and vendor maturity; and the CEO was the economic buyer and final decision-maker. Internal build and the status quo were the competitive paths. Internally, I coordinated Boxo Product and Engineering to answer only what we could support.
>
> The map is complete when every material decision and implementation risk has an owner, every powerful stakeholder has an engagement plan, and the Account Director can see where access, proof or support is still missing. It remains a living hypothesis throughout the deal.

**Decision-role map:**

| Role | Core question |
|---|---|
| Business or workflow owner | Is the problem consequential and worth solving? |
| Champion | Can the internal case and stakeholder path be mobilized? |
| Economic buyer | Should the business authorize the investment and risk? |
| Product and technical evaluators | Can the solution work in the required experience and environment? |
| Security, privacy, compliance or governance | Can it operate within the required control boundaries? |
| Finance, Legal and Procurement | Can the economics and paper process be approved? |
| Implementation owner | Can the customer allocate capacity and make the solution operational? |
| End users or operators | Will the workflow work in practice and be adopted? |
| Blocker or competitive path | Who or what benefits from waiting, building or keeping the status quo? |

**Fields to track for each stakeholder:**

1. Decision role.
2. Desired business or operational outcome.
3. Primary objection or proof requirement.
4. Influence and ability to block.
5. Stance: supportive, neutral, skeptical, opposed or unknown.
6. Engagement state: inferred, identified, engaged or validated.
7. Relationship owner and next action.

C2 provides the workflow-centered OpenAI stakeholder-map frame. S3 lines 43–75 identifies the enterprise roles and staged decision scaffold. S1 lines 150–186 supports mapping executives, managers, users, Finance, Legal, Procurement and technical/risk roles by the questions they own.
