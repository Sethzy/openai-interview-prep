### 44. How do you respond when a prospect says they are not interested?

> I acknowledge it and ask one respectful diagnostic question rather than arguing. I want to understand whether “not interested” means poor fit, wrong person, weak timing, low priority or insufficient proof. I might say, “Understood—before I close the loop, is the issue that this is not a priority, or that my hypothesis about the workflow is wrong?” If I am wrong, I thank them and disqualify cleanly. If the fit is real but the timing is not, I ask what would make it relevant and move the account into monitored nurture. If the issue is proof, I only continue when I have something specific and close to their world. The goal is clarity, not overcoming every no.

**One-question diagnostic:**

1. Acknowledge the answer without resistance.
2. Diagnose once: **fit, person, priority, timing or proof?**
3. If the hypothesis is wrong, close the loop.
4. If timing is the issue, record the trigger that would justify reconnecting.
5. If proof is the issue, return only with relevant evidence—not another generic bump.

**Simple example:**

> “Understood. Before I close the loop, is the issue that cross-border payment reliability is already where it needs to be, or simply that changing providers is not a priority this quarter?”

### 46. How should AI change SDR prospecting?

> AI should increase an SDR's judgment density, not just message volume. I separate intelligence from judgment. AI can help build and deduplicate the account universe, collect source-backed signals, summarize what changed, suggest a stakeholder map, match proof points and draft an account brief or outreach option. The human still decides whether the account is worth interrupting, whether the signal is actually messageable, who owns the problem, what tone is appropriate and whether anything should be sent. I would also capture replies and objections so the playbook improves over time. The operating test is simple: does AI make me more prepared, specific and useful to the buyer, or does it merely let me produce generic outreach faster?

**Human-reviewed AI loop:**

1. Retrieve evidence from approved internal and external sources.
2. Separate verified facts, inferences and missing information.
3. Generate an account brief: **why this account, why now, why this buyer, why this workflow**.
4. Suggest—but do not automatically approve—priority, outreach and CRM changes.
5. Human-review customer claims, commercial fields, tone and the decision to send.
6. Feed replies, objections and outcomes back into the playbook.

**Simple output:**

### 47. How do you prepare for a first discovery call?

### 48. How do you set the agenda and establish credibility?

**Opening structure:**

2. State one evidence-backed hypothesis in the buyer's language.
3. Ask permission to test it and make it safe to say you are wrong.
4. Agree that the call should end with a clear next step or a clear no.

**Simple opening:**

### 49. How do you run first discovery?

> I run first discovery around six questions: what business priority is driving the conversation, how the workflow works today, where it breaks, what the consequence is, who owns the decision and what proof or next step would make sense. I start with my hypothesis, then follow the buyer's answers rather than forcing every qualification field into one call. With EU Holidays, the conversation widened from FX into payment reliability, month-end reconciliation, operational continuity during live tours and future destination coverage. I then mapped Finance, Operations, Commercial, the CFO and CEO. I finish by recapping the problem in the buyer's language and either earning a concrete next step with an owner and purpose or slowing the opportunity down.

**Discovery sequence:**

1. **Priority:** Why is this worth discussing now?
2. **Workflow:** How does the work happen today, across which people and systems?
3. **Gap and cause:** Where does it fail, and why has it remained unresolved?
4. **Consequence:** What becomes expensive, risky, delayed or constrained?
5. **People and process:** Who owns, evaluates, approves, implements and can block it?
6. **Proof and next step:** What must be true for the buyer to continue evaluating?

**Closing recap:**

> “It sounds like the priority is X, the current gap is Y because of Z, and the next proof you need is A. Did I capture that correctly?”

### 50. How do you uncover the cost of inaction?

> I do not manufacture pain or jump straight to an ROI number. At EU Holidays, payment reliability needed to move from approximately 80% to above 99%. Around 20% of payments were delayed by bank-side compliance issues, and approximately 2% were rejected outright, mostly for China. Month-end reconciliation also required five people for roughly two full days, with common errors and double-counting to investigate. The cost of inaction was therefore operational risk during live tours, recurring Finance effort and limits on which destinations the Commercial team could confidently support—not a made-up savings figure.

**Gap calculation:**

`Target → current reality → measurable shortfall → root cause → consequence of staying the same`

### 52. How do you qualify an opportunity?

> I qualify for five things: problem, priority, people, process and proof. Is there a specific workflow problem with a meaningful consequence? Is it tied to a current business priority and reason to act? Is there an accountable owner and a path to the economic, technical and risk stakeholders? Do we understand how the customer will evaluate and approve the decision? And can we agree on a bounded proof step with a measurable outcome? At EU Holidays, that meant validating reliability and reconciliation pain, developing the Head of Finance as champion, mapping Operations, Commercial, the CFO and CEO, and landing with a controlled FX scope rather than forcing a full-platform migration. Interest without those five elements is not yet a qualified enterprise opportunity.

**Five-part qualification:**

1. **Problem:** Named workflow, root cause and consequence.
2. **Priority:** Reason now, desired outcome and cost of delay.
3. **People:** Owner, champion, technical/risk stakeholders and economic buyer.
4. **Process:** Evaluation, approval, procurement and decision path.
5. **Proof:** Smallest credible validation step and success measure.
