### 74. How do you sell against an incumbent or a trusted bank?

> I do not begin by asking the customer to rip out something they already trust. I first understand what the incumbent does well, where the current workflow still breaks and whether that gap is important enough to justify change.
>
> If it is, I isolate a narrow workflow where we can create measurable value without forcing an unnecessary migration. I align with the buyer on what the new approach must prove, keep the existing provider as a fallback where appropriate and let customer evidence—not a broad superiority claim—earn the next expansion.
>
> That was the approach with EU Holidays. Their established banks carried trust, but the payment workflow was producing delays, rejected payments and significant reconciliation work. I did not ask them to move everything. We started with FX and controlled volume, used relevant proof from nearby travel partners and tested whether Airwallex could perform reliably in EU Holidays' own operation. Once that worked, the Head of Finance had evidence to support broader adoption.
>
> My principle is: respect the incumbent, isolate the gap, reduce the commitment, prove the workflow and earn expansion.

**Incumbent-to-expansion framework:**

1. **Respect:** Establish why the incumbent is trusted and which jobs it performs well.
2. **Isolate:** Identify the specific workflow, consequence or strategic need that remains unresolved.
3. **Reduce:** Design the smallest commercially meaningful coexistence wedge.
4. **Prove:** Agree on buyer-owned evidence that would make the new approach credible.
5. **Expand:** Broaden the relationship only after realized value reduces switching risk.

**Simple example:**

> “I would not ask you to replace a trusted provider before we have earned that right. Which specific workflow is still underperforming, and what would a controlled test have to prove for you to move more volume?”

### 75. How do you handle a prospect already using a competitor?

> Existing competitor usage is context, not an automatic disqualification and not a reason to attack the current provider. I want to understand four things: what job the customer hired it to do, what is working well, where the current approach falls short and what has changed enough for them to consider another option.
>
> From there, there are three rational outcomes. We may coexist around a distinct workflow, we may earn a displacement case if the customer has a material unresolved gap, or we may decide there is no reason to change and nurture the account. I would rather reach the third answer honestly than manufacture differentiation.
>
> I also treat internal build and the status quo as competitors. At Boxo, Binance ultimately chose to build internally because it had the engineering resources. My mistake was mistaking an engaged contact for a committed buying process and not establishing early enough which evidence could change the build-versus-buy decision. That loss taught me to qualify the competitive process, economic buyer and decision criteria—not merely the buyer's interest in learning from us.
>
> In an OpenAI conversation, I would use the same commercial posture. I would identify the named workflow, business outcome, gaps and decision criteria before suggesting coexistence or change. If the comparison depends on current technical capabilities, security, pricing or packaging, I would use verified official material and bring in the appropriate technical or account partner rather than improvising a claim.

**Competitive-discovery questions:**

1. What is the current solution being used for today?
2. What does the customer value about it and want to preserve?
3. Which workflow, user group or outcome remains underserved?
4. Why is the customer reconsidering the status quo now?
5. What evidence and decision criteria would justify coexistence or change?
6. Who owns the final technical, financial and strategic decision?

| Route | When it is credible |
|---|---|

### 76. How do you prepare for and handle objections?

> I prepare for objections before the call, but I do not assume the first objection is the real one. My structure is: clarify, validate, answer with relevant evidence and confirm what remains unresolved.
>
> First, I ask enough questions to classify the concern. Is it poor fit, low priority, missing value, implementation effort, technical feasibility, trust, risk, price or simply timing? I acknowledge why the concern is reasonable, then respond only to that issue using a precise answer, nearby proof or a defined validation step. Finally, I ask whether that resolves the concern or exposes a second question.
>
> At Boxo, UNA's central concern was not price. It was build versus buy combined with vendor maturity. I did not tell Engineering that it could not build the platform. I compared the full operating burden: launch speed, engineering capacity, maintenance across multiple integrations and displaced roadmap work. I then brought Boxo's Product and Engineering teams into the process, answered the lightweight RFP and stayed explicit about what was supported, who owned implementation and where the boundaries were.
>
> I also feed repeated objections back into the operating system. At Airwallex, I built a 51-page sales FAQ and playbook covering discovery, product explanations, common objections, competitor positioning and next-step language. The goal was not to memorize rebuttals. It was to make the next seller better prepared to diagnose the concern and bring the right proof.

**Objection-handling loop:**

1. **Prepare:** Anticipate objections by persona, stage and workflow.
2. **Clarify:** Ask what is behind the stated concern and why it matters.
3. **Validate:** Show that the concern is commercially or technically reasonable.
4. **Respond:** Use the smallest relevant answer, proof point or test.
5. **Confirm:** Ask whether the concern is resolved and what remains open.
6. **Learn:** Capture the objection, evidence used and result in the playbook.

**Simple example:**

> “Building internally may be completely rational. Can I ask what you are optimizing for—control, launch speed, engineering cost or long-term maintenance? Then we can compare the two paths on the criteria that actually matter to your team.”

### 77. How do you communicate credibly when the buyer is more technical than you?

> I do not try to prove credibility by pretending to be the strongest engineer in the room. I earn it through preparation, precise questions, accurate summaries and honest boundaries.
>
> I need to understand the business workflow and enough of the mechanism to ask useful questions: which systems are involved, where data and permissions come from, what must integrate, what happens when the workflow fails and who owns implementation and support. I then play back what I heard in plain language so the buyer can correct me.
>
> If the question goes beyond my depth, I say what I know, what I do not know and who should validate it. I bring in the right Product, Engineering or solutions specialist with a clear brief, remain accountable for the follow-up and translate the answer back to the business decision. That is stronger than bluffing or disappearing behind the technical team.
>
> At UNA, Engineering had legitimate questions about feasibility, integration, ownership and whether to build internally. I translated those concerns into precise questions for Boxo's Product and Engineering teams, coordinated their involvement and stayed honest about supported scope. I owned the commercial thread; the technical specialists owned the deep validation.

**Credibility framework:**

1. **Workflow:** Understand what the buyer is trying to accomplish and where the current process breaks.
2. **Mechanism:** Understand the systems, data, integration, permissions, failure path and operating owner at the level required for discovery.
3. **Boundary:** State what is known, what requires validation and which specialist owns the answer.
4. **Translation:** Connect the technical answer back to speed, risk, cost, control or the desired business outcome.
5. **Closure:** Return with the answer, source and next technical decision—do not leave the question hanging.

**Simple language in the room:**

## VIII. Business Case, Forecasting And Deal Control

### 78. How do you build a business case?

> I build the business case with the customer throughout discovery, not as a late-stage ROI slide. The core structure is: executive priority, current state, desired state, quantified gap, cause, consequence of doing nothing, proposed bridge, proof required, implementation and risk, and commercial investment.
>
> I keep one value spine but translate it by stakeholder. The executive needs to understand why this matters now and how it affects growth, cost or risk. The workflow owner needs a better operating outcome. Technical teams need a credible implementation path. Finance, Security and Legal need assumptions, controls and accountability they can defend.
>
> EU Holidays is a strong example. The current payment-reliability baseline was approximately 80%, against a desired state above 99%. Month-end reconciliation required five people for roughly two full days and involved common errors and double-counting investigations. Finance cared about reliability, economics, reconciliation and control; Operations cared about live-tour continuity; Commercial cared about destination coverage; and the CFO and CEO cared about growth and resilience without unacceptable switching risk.
>
> The business case became credible because the initial decision was bounded: controlled FX volume could prove product and support reliability before a wider expansion. A strong case explains not only why the future is attractive, but why the current path is costly and why the proposed path is safe enough to choose.

**One-page business-case structure:**

1. **Priority:** Which executive or operating objective does this support?
2. **Current state:** How does the workflow operate today?
3. **Target state:** What should improve, by how much and by when?
4. **Gap and cause:** What is preventing that result today?
5. **Consequence:** What happens if the current path continues?
6. **Bridge and proof:** What scoped approach could close the gap, and what must it prove?
7. **Implementation and risk:** Who owns rollout, dependencies, controls and failure handling?
8. **Investment and decision:** What commitment is required, and how will the customer decide?

**EU Holidays value spine:**

| Stakeholder | Decision value |
|---|---|
| Finance | Payment reliability, reconciliation, economics and control |
| Operations | Overseas payments succeeding while tours are live |
| Commercial | Greater confidence supporting additional destinations |
| CFO and CEO | Growth and operating resilience without unacceptable switching risk |

### 79. How do you quantify business value when the customer has incomplete data?

> I do not solve incomplete data by creating false precision. I separate what is known, what the customer can estimate and what remains unknown, then build a transparent range rather than a single heroic ROI number.
>
> I start with the smallest decision model the buyer can own. We agree on the formula, label every assumption and use low, base and high cases where an input is uncertain. I then test which assumptions actually change the decision. If the result depends on one unknown—such as workflow volume, loaded labor cost or failure consequence—that becomes a discovery or pilot measurement requirement rather than a number I invent.
>
> At EU Holidays, we knew month-end reconciliation involved five people for roughly two full days. We did not have a verified loaded hourly cost or exact productive hours per day. So the defensible model would be: people multiplied by hours per close, closes per year and loaded cost, then multiplied by the percentage of work the new process can demonstrably remove. Payment reliability should be modelled separately using confirmed payment volume, delay frequency and customer-owned consequence data.
>
> The purpose of the model is not to make the return look large. It is to show which facts are already strong enough to support a decision, which assumptions the customer accepts and what evidence the controlled rollout still needs to produce.

**Incomplete-data method:**

| Evidence class | How I treat it |
|---|---|
| Confirmed | Use the customer's measured baseline and cite the owner/source |
| Estimated | Show the assumption, owner and low/base/high range |
| Unknown | Leave it blank or turn it into a discovery/pilot measurement |
| Non-financial | Keep risk, control and strategic capacity explicit rather than forcing every benefit into dollars |

**Illustrative reconciliation formula:**

`annual hours = people × hours per close × closes per year`

`annual capacity value = annual hours × loaded hourly cost × proven reduction percentage`

**Sensitivity test:**

> “Even at the conservative case, does the operational and risk improvement justify the scoped next step? If not, which unverified assumption would have to change?”
