# Draft Review: Q27–34 — Territory, Account Priority And Market Strategy

This is a review draft only. It does not modify the canonical interview pack.

### 27. Why qualify workflows rather than logos alone?

> Because the company tells me the potential, but the workflow tells me whether there is actually a deal.
>
> A large enterprise can contain dozens of possible AI use cases, each with a different owner, business consequence, technical environment and path to approval. If I qualify only the logo, I still cannot tell the Account Director what problem we are solving, who owns it, why it matters now or what the customer would need to prove.
>
> At the workflow level, I can establish the current process, the cost or risk of leaving it unchanged, the accountable owner, the reason to act, the success measure and the smallest credible proof step. That turns general AI interest into something commercially actionable.
>
> I used the same logic at Airwallex. EU Holidays was not attractive merely because it was a travel company. Its advertised European and China tours suggested specific payment corridors. Discovery then confirmed a finance workflow with material reliability and reconciliation problems: payment performance was around 80% against a desired state above 99%, and month-end reconciliation required five people for roughly two days. The Head of Finance owned the problem, and controlled FX volume gave us a safe first proof step.
>
> For OpenAI, I would rather give an AD a clearly owned workflow with measurable pain, stakeholder access and a credible evaluation path than a more famous logo whose innovation team is only interested in discussing AI. Company-level fit determines territory potential; workflow evidence determines whether the account is ready to become qualified pipeline.

**Workflow-level qualification: problem → consequence → owner → reason now → success measure → proof step.**

**Short version if interrupted:**

> The logo tells me the potential; the workflow tells me whether there is a deal. A workflow gives me the problem, consequence, owner, urgency, success measure and proof step. Without those, I have an attractive company and general interest. With them, I have something an Account Director can actually advance.

### 28. How do you tier accounts?

> I use two layers: structural fit tells me whether an account belongs in the territory, and an action score tells me how much seller effort it deserves now.
>
> I score the account out of ten. Potential commercial value is worth three points. Strength of the workflow and fit with what we can credibly solve is worth two. Ability to act—ownership, technical readiness and a plausible governance path—is worth two. Evidence of timing is worth two. Stakeholder access and proof proximity are worth one.
>
> Accounts scoring eight to ten become Tier 1. They receive bespoke one-to-one research, a stakeholder map and close partnership with the AD. Accounts scoring five to seven become Tier 2. They fit the ICP but one or two conditions are weaker, so I work them through a focused one-to-few campaign around a shared workflow while monitoring for stronger signals. Accounts below five receive scaled coverage, nurture or no current action until something changes.
>
> The score is a decision aid, not false precision. Before discovery, several inputs are hypotheses. Once the buyer confirms the workflow, economics, stakeholders and timing, I update the score and may move the account between tiers.
>
> At Boxo, UNA was a clear Tier 1 account because it combined scale, platform fit, an active eSIM expansion, an identifiable Head of Partnerships and a first use case that could expand into more merchant categories. A similarly large fintech with no current initiative or owner could still belong in the ICP, but it would not deserve the same manual effort that week.

**Ten-point account-priority score:**

| Criterion | Points | What I am testing |
|---|---:|---|
| Potential value | 0–3 | Scale, strategic value and credible expansion potential |
| Workflow strength and solution fit | 0–2 | Important problem that maps to a supportable starting point |
| Ability to act | 0–2 | Accountable owner, implementation readiness and governance path |
| Reason now | 0–2 | Active initiative, deadline, usage, mandate, cost pressure or other compelling event |
| Access and proof proximity | 0–1 | Warm path, reachable buyer or relevant evidence the account can trust |
| **Total** | **0–10** | Determines present seller effort, not permanent account value |

| Score | Tier | Seller motion |
|---:|---|---|
| 8–10 | Tier 1 | Bespoke one-to-one account plan with the AD |
| 5–7 | Tier 2 | Targeted one-to-few campaign plus signal monitoring |
| 0–4 | Tier 3 / nurture | Scaled coverage, events, inbound, monitoring or no present outreach |

### 29. How do you prioritize accounts inside the same tier?

> The tier sets the level of investment; the daily priority comes from which account has the strongest reason to act and the clearest path to a meaningful next step now.
>
> Inside a tier, I look at five things: the freshness and offer-specificity of the signal, the strength of the executive or workflow priority, the closeness of the available proof, access to the right stakeholder and whether I have enough capacity to do the account justice. I am asking, “Which account can I responsibly advance this week?” rather than, “Which logo looks best on a list?”
>
> UNA is the clean example. It was one of several large fintech platforms that fit Boxo's ICP. What moved it to the front was its public eSIM expansion. That signal identified a current initiative and likely owner. I then researched the supply side and spoke with relevant eSIM providers, giving me a useful point of view and a stronger reason for the Head of Partnerships to respond. The combination of timing, access and proof made UNA more actionable than an equally large wallet with no active category expansion.
>
> Operationally, I keep a small daily queue: the best three people or accounts to contact today, a top-ten monitor list and a do-not-touch-yet list with the missing evidence recorded. If a monitored account announces a relevant launch, an executive raises the priority or a warm path appears, it can move ahead immediately. If a Tier 1 account loses its owner or timing, it can move down without changing its long-term potential.

**Within-tier tie-breaker:** fresh signal → active priority → close proof → stakeholder access → capacity to execute well.

### 30. If you joined OpenAI tomorrow, which companies would you target first and why?

> Before naming accounts, I would confirm the territory, current ownership, existing customer relationships, product signals and the AD's priorities. Assuming these companies were in scope, the first three I would pressure-test are Grab, DBS and FairPrice Group—not because they are famous, but because each has a public workflow signal and teaches us a different enterprise motion.
>
> **Grab** would be a product-led expansion account, not a greenfield cold account. Grab has publicly described work with OpenAI on its Merchant AI Assistant and Driver Companion. I would align with the account team on where adoption is already working, then investigate the next measurable workflow across merchant operations, driver support, internal data access or regional rollout. The question would be where existing proof can expand, not whether Grab is interested in AI.
>
> **DBS** would be a regulated coexistence account. Its 2025 reporting describes more than 430 AI use cases, DBS-GPT, customer-support workflows and coding assistance. I would not approach it with “use AI.” I would identify a workflow where current tools leave a measurable gap, map the business, technical and governance owners and determine whether OpenAI can coexist with or improve the current approach. Its sophistication raises the bar, but it also makes the buying conversation concrete.
>
> **FairPrice Group** would be a retail workflow account. Its public materials describe generative-AI shopping assistance, AI-enabled store operations and plans to improve customer-support availability and efficiency. I would test a narrow workflow such as store knowledge, frontline operations or customer support, with clear user, quality and adoption measures.
>
> This gives the AD three different learning lanes: expand a known OpenAI relationship, earn relevance inside a mature regulated AI estate and create demand around an observable retail workflow. I would then rank them using internal usage, ownership, timing and account-status data that I do not have today.

### 31. Which APAC market would you enter first, and why?

> I would start with Singapore, but as a regional proof market rather than simply the largest TAM.
>
> I would compare markets across six factors: economic value, AI readiness, workflow urgency, access to decision-makers, sales friction and the reference or expansion value of an early win. Singapore scores well because it concentrates regulated financial institutions, regional headquarters and cross-border operating teams in a market where enterprise buyers can become references for the wider region.
>
> The timing is also unusually concrete. OpenAI announced OpenAI for Singapore in May 2026, including an Applied AI Lab—the company's first outside the United States—and a focus on public service, finance, healthcare and digital infrastructure. That gives a seller a credible local deployment narrative and an ecosystem in which regulated buyers, technical talent and public-sector adoption are already part of the conversation.
>
> I would still choose the first campaign at the workflow level. I might begin with a governed financial-services customer-operations workflow, a developer-productivity motion or a retail-operations use case, depending on which internal OpenAI proof points and account signals are strongest. The initial goal would be a small number of referenceable wins and a repeatable buyer map, then use that learning to decide whether the next expansion market should be Australia, Japan or a Southeast Asian market.
>
> Singapore has a limitation: its domestic market is smaller than several APAC economies. So success cannot mean maximizing local logo count. It means building trusted proof, learning the regulated buying process and creating a regional playbook that travels selectively.

**Market-entry test:** value pool → readiness → urgency → buyer access → friction → reference and expansion value.

### 32. What is different about prospecting in APAC versus globally?

> The sales principles are the same globally: earn attention with relevance, find a valuable workflow, map the buying group and create a credible next step. What changes in APAC is that the inputs vary sharply by market, so I would never use “APAC” as one persona or one sequence.
>
> I localize five things. First is the market thesis: the priority workflow in a Singapore bank may be different from that of an Indonesian platform or a Vietnamese technology company. Second is the buyer map: regional strategy, procurement or governance may sit in Singapore, while the operational owner and implementation team sit in another country. Third is trust and proof: some buyers need a local reference, partner or in-person conversation before a remote case study feels relevant. Fourth is execution: language, preferred channel, events and relationship paths can differ. Fifth is economics and governance: labor costs, budget ownership, data requirements and procurement processes can materially change the business case.
>
> Boxo taught me this directly. Across qualified opportunities including Zalo, GCash, DANA, Paytm, TrueMoney and Touch 'n Go, the initiative owner was not reliably one title. Sometimes Partnerships drove it, sometimes Product, sometimes Engineering, and the clearest accounts had a dedicated mini-app team. My job was to map the initiative before choosing the persona.
>
> I also used travel selectively. I would tell priority stakeholders I was planning to be in their city, confirm enough valuable meetings and then book the trip. That created in-person trust without treating travel as activity for its own sake.
>
> So my APAC playbook would have a common operating system—fit, signal, workflow, stakeholder, proof and next step—but country-specific evidence, language, relationships and business cases. I would validate those differences through local customers, partners and the AD rather than relying on national stereotypes.

**Five localization tests:** market thesis, buyer map, trust and proof, execution channel, economics and governance.

The comprehensive answer bank supports the “APAC is not one market” frame. The JD requires APAC stakeholder mapping and technically complex, regulated-account work.

### 33. How would you decide which vertical or campaign to run first?

> I would start with proof proximity, not the biggest theoretical TAM.
>
> I would ask six questions. Do we already have a successful customer or internal proof point close to this workflow? Is the pain valuable and repeated across a finite set of accounts? Can I observe signals before outreach? Can I identify and reach the likely owner? Is there a narrow first use case the buyer can evaluate? And will the campaign teach us something reusable quickly?
>
> I would then test the motion manually with a small named-account set. The playbook would include the account rules, anti-ICP, buyer map, signal definitions, proof point, message, discovery questions and handoff standard. Replies, objections and meetings would update the thesis before we scale the campaign.
>
> That is how I approached travel at Airwallex. I had early evidence from Chorus calls with the more mature UK team that travel could be a successful vertical. In Singapore, brochures and ecommerce pages made outbound-tour destinations visible, which let me form payment-corridor hypotheses before a meeting. The buyer was usually identifiable in Finance, the pain—reliability, reconciliation and cross-border coverage—was repeatable, and controlled FX volume gave us a low-risk proof step. Once the early accounts worked, partner proof and customer evidence made later conversations easier.
>
> For OpenAI, I would begin with the strongest approved internal customer proof and look for APAC accounts with the same workflow, buyer pressure and governance pattern. If customer operations has the best proof, I would not simultaneously launch a generic “AI for banks” campaign. I would run a narrow customer-operations campaign into the accounts where support volume, knowledge complexity, owner access and proof criteria are visible.

**Campaign-selection gates:** close proof → repeated valuable pain → observable signals → reachable owner → bounded proof step → reusable learning.

### 34. How do you decide whether to contact, monitor, nurture or disqualify an account?

> I separate fit from timing, then route the account according to what is missing.
>
> I contact now when the account fits, there is an offer-specific signal or active workflow, I can identify a plausible owner and I have a relevant point of view or proof step. I monitor when the structural fit is strong but there is no respectful reason to interrupt the buyer yet; the account stays on a watchlist for usage, hiring, launches, executive changes, events or another meaningful trigger.
>
> I nurture when there is already a relationship or confirmed interest, but timing, readiness or stakeholder coverage is incomplete. Nurture has a reason, owner and revisit date—it is not an endless marketing bucket. I might share a relevant case, invite the buyer to an event or check back against the specific milestone they named.
>
> I disqualify when the structural problem is real: no valuable workflow, no credible OpenAI fit, an unsupported requirement, no ability to act or no realistic path to value. I record the reason so the account can be reconsidered if the underlying condition changes.
>
> UNA shows the difference. Before its eSIM initiative, it could have been a strong-fit account to monitor. The public expansion signal, identifiable Head of Partnerships and relevant supply-side work moved it into contact now. If discovery had shown that the project was only exploratory, that Engineering rejected the integration model or that UNA wanted extensive custom work without commercial commitment, I would have slowed or disqualified the opportunity.
>
> Operationally, I want a small visible system: today's top three, a top-ten monitor list, a nurture queue with dated triggers and a do-not-touch list with explicit reasons. That protects buyer attention and keeps the AD able to inspect my judgment.

**Fit-and-timing routing:**

| Route | Condition | Next action |
|---|---|---|
| Contact now | Strong fit, current messageable signal, plausible owner and useful point of view | Bespoke outreach or immediate AD-aligned follow-up |
| Monitor | Strong fit, weak timing and no current relationship | Watch defined signals; do not manufacture urgency |
| Nurture | Confirmed interest or relationship, but readiness/timing/stakeholder gap | Provide relevant value and set a dated revisit trigger |
| Disqualify | Structural mismatch, unsupported need or no credible path to value | Record the reason; reopen only if the condition changes |
