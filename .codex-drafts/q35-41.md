### 35. How do you research a strategic account?

> I research backward from the decision I need to make: why this account, why now, which workflow, which stakeholder and what next action is justified.
>
> I start with internal context because the account may already have product usage, prior conversations, an existing relationship, closed-lost history or Account Director knowledge. Then I research the company externally: its business model, current priorities, products, customers, operating footprint, leadership language, hiring, launches, partnerships, regulatory pressures and relevant technology choices.
>
> I turn that evidence into a one-page account brief with seven fields: the company priority, workflow hypothesis, business consequence, timing signal, provisional stakeholder map, closest proof point and likely objections. I keep verified facts separate from inferences and explicitly record what discovery still needs to test.
>
> EU Holidays is a good example. I first used UK-team Chorus calls to understand why travel could be a strong Airwallex vertical. I then collected EU Holidays' brochures at a travel fair, identified its concentration in Europe and China tours and mapped those destinations to the likely payment corridors. That gave me a hypothesis around European payment economics, China payment reliability and reconciliation. Before approaching the office, I had also mapped Finance, Operations, Commercial, the CFO and CEO, prepared a tailored deck and built a preliminary ROI calculator.
>
> The output of research is not a large dossier. It is a decision-ready brief: contact now, investigate further, monitor, route to the Account Director or deprioritize—and the evidence explaining why.

**Strategic-account brief:**

| Field | Question to answer |
|---|---|
| Business priority | What is the company trying to change or achieve? |
| Workflow | Which concrete process may be affected? |
| Consequence | What could be slow, costly, risky or strategically constrained? |
| Why now | What changed, and is it a timing signal or merely company fit? |
| Stakeholder map | Who owns, uses, validates, funds, governs and can block the workflow? |
| Proof match | Which evidence is closest by workflow, role, region and risk? |
| Next action | Contact, research, monitor, route or deprioritize? |

**Research discipline:**

1. Check internal account history and coordinate with the Account Director.
2. Retrieve external evidence from primary and credible sources.
3. Separate verified facts, hypotheses and missing information.
4. Build the workflow and stakeholder hypotheses before writing outreach.
5. End with a recommended action, not a pile of research.

### 36. How do you turn a trigger into a non-obvious point of view?

> I treat the trigger as a routing signal, not as the message. A funding announcement, new executive, hiring plan, product launch or expansion tells me where to investigate. Repeating the announcement back to the buyer does not add value.
>
> I work through a simple implication chain: what changed, which workflow that change affects, what new constraint or opportunity it may create, who owns that consequence and what hypothesis I can credibly test. I then look for evidence that either strengthens or disproves the hypothesis and match it to a relevant proof point.
>
> At UNA, the public eSIM expansion was the trigger. The non-obvious work was researching the category, identifying relevant eSIM providers and speaking with the supply side before approaching the account. That changed the point of view from “I saw you are launching eSIMs” to “Launching the first category is only one challenge; UNA may need a repeatable way to source, integrate and launch additional services without creating a bespoke engineering project each time.”
>
> I would take that into outreach as a hypothesis, not a diagnosis. The buyer should be able to correct me. A strong point of view is specific enough to be useful, but humble enough to be tested.

**Trigger-to-POV chain:**

`Trigger → business change → affected workflow → likely consequence → accountable owner → testable hypothesis → relevant proof`

**UNA example:**

| Layer | Evidence or inference |
|---|---|
| Trigger | UNA signaled an eSIM expansion initiative |
| Investigation | Relevant eSIM providers and the supply side |
| Immediate challenge | Secure and launch the first category |
| Wider implication | Repeated categories could become repeated integrations |
| Likely owner | Head of Partnerships, with Product and Engineering involved |
| Point of view | The first launch could become a reusable embedded-services model |
| Discovery test | Is this a one-off launch or a broader platform-expansion priority? |

### 37. What makes a signal messageable?

> A signal is messageable when it creates a specific and respectful reason to speak to a particular person about a workflow they plausibly own. Truth alone is not enough. A company can raise funding, hire AI engineers or announce a new strategy and still not deserve outreach today.
>
> I use six tests. First, does the account fit? Second, is the signal recent and strong enough to affect timing? Third, can I identify the person or group likely to own the consequence? Fourth, can I connect the signal to a concrete workflow rather than a generic category? Fifth, is there a meaningful business implication? Sixth, do I have credible evidence, proof or a useful question that earns the interruption?
>
> EU Holidays' brochures are a good example. “You sell tours” was true but not messageable. The signal became useful only when I saw the Europe and China concentration, mapped it to relevant payment corridors and formed a hypothesis about payment economics, reliability and reconciliation for the Head of Finance.
>
> If the signal fails the test, I do not force an email. I either investigate further, monitor the account for a stronger reason or deprioritize it. Strategic outbound includes knowing what not to send.

**Six-part messageability test:**

1. **Fit:** Is the account worth pursuing?
2. **Timing:** Does the signal change when action may matter?
3. **Owner:** Is there a named person or stakeholder group likely to care?
4. **Workflow:** Can I identify the process affected?
5. **Consequence:** Is there a plausible cost, risk, growth or strategic implication?
6. **Credibility:** Do I have evidence, relevant proof or a useful question—and would I be comfortable explaining the source?

**Routing rule:**

| Result | Action |
|---|---|
| All six are strong | Contact now with human-reviewed outreach |
| Strong fit, weak timing or owner | Monitor or investigate further |
| Strong signal, weak workflow connection | Do not use it as outreach yet |
| Weak fit or no credible implication | Deprioritize |

**Raw-source basis:** C1 establishes the EU Holidays destination-to-corridor reasoning. C2 provides the messageability checklist and the distinction between true and messageable signals. QMD-retrieved *AI-Native Account Intelligence* (#5536ad, lines 47–86) supports separating signals from scoring and testing offer-specific interpretation, crowdedness, buyer language and timing. The Career Ops enterprise-sales playbook (#fd09fa, lines 31–68 and 214–264) supports territory stewardship and routing accounts into contact, monitor or leave-alone states.

> [!warning] Truth boundary
> Messageability is a pre-outreach judgment, not proof that the buyer has the problem. Do not convert public evidence into an unsupported customer claim or imply that every high-fit strategic account should be contacted immediately.

### 38. How do you map stakeholders before outreach?

> I build a provisional stakeholder map around the workflow and decision path, not around an org chart full of senior titles.
>
> Before outreach, I want a hypothesis for who owns the business outcome, who feels the day-to-day problem, who could champion change, who validates technical fit, who owns security or governance, who controls budget and procurement, who implements the solution and who can block it. I also look for warm paths and any dedicated transformation, AI, platform or mini-app team that may consolidate ownership.
>
> I mark every person as confirmed, inferred or unknown. That matters because a title is only a clue. In Boxo accounts, the same platform-expansion initiative could be owned by Partnerships, Product, Engineering or a dedicated mini-app team. The map helped me choose the most likely entry point, but discovery determined whether the assumption was right.
>
> For UNA, my initial hypothesis put Partnerships closest to the eSIM supply and launch initiative, with Product and Engineering as likely validators. As the deal developed, the confirmed map widened into legal, compliance and risk, with the CEO as the final decision-maker. The pre-outreach map gave me a place to start; buyer evidence completed it.
>
> The purpose is not to contact everyone at once. It is to understand which person to approach first, which stakeholders must eventually believe and what evidence each will need.

**Pre-outreach stakeholder map:**

| Decision role | What I need to understand |
|---|---|
| Workflow owner | Owns the initiative, metric or operating problem |
| Day-to-day user | Experiences the workflow and can correct the hypothesis |
| Potential champion | Has incentive, influence and a reason to help navigate |
| Technical evaluator | Validates systems, data, integration, feasibility and ownership |
| Security/governance/risk | Evaluates permissions, controls, data boundaries and organizational risk |
| Economic buyer | Can authorize the business decision and defend the investment |
| Procurement/legal/finance | Controls the buying and contractual path |
| Implementer/adoption owner | Must make the change work after signature |
| Blocker or incumbent owner | May lose control, budget or political capital if the account changes |

**Mapping rule:**

> Map broadly enough to understand the decision; engage narrowly enough to preserve trust.

### 39. Who do you target first inside an account?

> I target the person closest to the urgent problem who has enough ownership to create internal movement. I do not automatically start with the most senior title.
>
> I look for three things: responsibility for the active initiative, a measurable consequence if it stalls and a credible path to the other people needed for a decision. A senior executive may have authority but little workflow detail. An end user may understand the pain but lack influence. The strongest first contact is usually the accountable workflow owner who can become a credible champion.
>
> At UNA, that was the Head of Partnerships because the immediate problem was securing and launching the eSIM partner category. In other Boxo accounts, the owner could sit in Product, Engineering or a dedicated mini-app team. At EU Holidays, I chose the Head of Finance because Finance owned payment cost, reliability, reconciliation and control; Operations and Commercial felt the downstream impact, while the CFO and CEO made the final decision.
>
> I use the first conversation to validate the map. If I have the wrong person, a relevant hypothesis still lets me ask a useful routing question: “Who owns this outcome internally?” The goal is to enter through ownership and then widen access deliberately—not spray the same message across the org chart.

**First-contact hierarchy:**

1. Dedicated owner of the active initiative or workflow.
2. Business or functional leader accountable for the affected metric.
3. Technical owner when the initiative is primarily developer-led or build-versus-buy.
4. Executive sponsor when the change is already an explicit strategic mandate.
5. High-context user or warm path who can validate the problem and route me correctly.

**Two verified examples:**

| Account | First stakeholder | Why |
|---|---|---|
| UNA Financial | Head of Partnerships | Owned the eSIM partner-supply and launch initiative |
| EU Holidays | Head of Finance | Owned payments, reliability, reconciliation and financial control |

### 40. How does your message change by stakeholder?

> I keep one account thesis but translate it into the outcome, metric and risk each stakeholder owns. If I send the same message to the CEO, Product, Engineering and Legal, I have not really mapped the account.
>
> Executives hear the strategic goal, economic consequence and risk of inaction. Functional leaders hear the metric and operating constraint they are accountable for. End users hear the day-to-day workflow and what becomes easier or more reliable. Technical buyers hear architecture, data, integration, evaluation and ownership. Security, legal and governance teams hear permissions, controls, responsibility and what must be true for safe adoption.
>
> At UNA, the Head of Partnerships heard faster partner sourcing and merchant launches. Product heard a coherent embedded experience that could extend beyond one category. Engineering heard a concrete build-versus-buy and maintenance question. Legal, compliance and risk heard explicit data, service and responsibility boundaries. The CEO heard faster platform expansion and monetization without making every new category dependent on internal hiring and bespoke development.
>
> The message changes, but the underlying case does not. Every stakeholder should be able to recognize their part in the same business decision.

**One value spine, multiple translations:**

| Stakeholder | Lead with | Avoid leading with |
|---|---|---|
| Executive | Strategic objective, economic value, speed and risk | Feature inventory |
| Functional owner | Metric, operating gap and desired state | Broad transformation language |
| End user/operator | Current workflow, friction, review and handoff | Executive-level ROI abstractions |
| Product | User experience, roadmap fit and adoption | Generic technical superiority |
| Engineering/technical | Feasibility, architecture, integration, maintenance and proof | Unsupported certainty or business-only claims |
| Security/legal/governance | Data boundaries, permissions, controls, auditability and responsibility | “Trust us” assurances |
| Finance/procurement | Why now, value basis, alternatives, implementation burden and buying path | A discount before value is established |

**Message-design question:**

> What does this person need to believe, in the language of the outcome they own, before they will help the account move?

### 41. What does a strong first-touch message look like?

> A strong first touch proves I did enough work to earn about 15 seconds. It contains one relevant observation, one non-obvious implication, one credibility point and one low-friction question.
>
> I keep it short enough that the buyer can understand why them, why now and why this conversation without opening a deck. The observation has to be specific and sourceable. The implication should connect to a workflow or business outcome the person plausibly owns. The proof should be close to their world rather than a famous but irrelevant logo. The question should let them correct the hypothesis, route me to the right owner or decide whether a conversation is worthwhile.
>
> I do not try to explain the whole company, list every capability or manufacture familiarity. For a strategic account, I would rather send one human-reviewed message with a clear account thesis than five generic “bumps.”

**First-touch structure:**

1. **Observation:** The specific, explainable evidence that caught my attention.
2. **Implication:** What that evidence may mean for the buyer's workflow or priority.
3. **Credibility:** Relevant research, proof or pattern—not a logo dump.
4. **Question:** A small, useful next step that makes correction easy.

**Representative UNA LinkedIn message:**

> Hi [Name]—I saw UNA was expanding into eSIMs. I have been speaking with relevant providers in the category, and one pattern is that sourcing the first partner is only part of the work; the next challenge is creating a repeatable way to launch additional services without rebuilding the integration each time. That is the problem we work on at Boxo. Is that initiative owned by Partnerships on your side, or would Product be the better team to speak with?

**Why the example works:**

| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Observation | UNA's eSIM expansion |
| Implication | First launch may expose a repeatability and integration problem |
| Credibility | Seth had already spoken with relevant providers |
| Question | Validates ownership without forcing a meeting |

**Shorter interview version:**

> One signal, one workflow hypothesis, one relevant proof point and one small question. The first touch should earn a reply, not attempt to complete the sale.
