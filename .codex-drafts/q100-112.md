## IX. Operating System And Behavioral Questions

### 100. What do you do when you are behind target?

> I diagnose the constraint before I change the activity.
>
> First, I separate coverage from conversion. Coverage asks whether I have enough appropriate accounts, stakeholders and activity to create the required pipeline. Conversion asks where the motion is breaking: account to response, response to meeting, meeting to qualified opportunity, or qualified opportunity to the next buyer milestone.
>
> Then I inspect the largest gap. If I am not reaching enough of the right buyers and the message is converting, I increase focused activity. If activity is healthy but response is weak, I examine account selection, stakeholder choice, signal strength, message or channel. If meetings are happening but not qualifying, I inspect whether I am targeting curiosity instead of a real workflow, owner and reason to act.
>
> I would bring my manager and Account Director into that diagnosis early. I would show them the data, state what I think is broken, choose one or two changes and agree on what we will inspect the following week. That makes the recovery coachable rather than emotional.
>
> The lesson I learned from running my own company is that you cannot rebuild pipeline only when you suddenly need it. I once allowed delivery and active customer work to crowd out consistent prospecting. When churn arrived, I had to rebuild from too close to zero. Since then, I have protected weekly pipeline generation even when live work feels more urgent.

**Coverage-versus-conversion diagnosis:**

| Symptom | What I inspect first | Likely response |
|---|---|---|
| Too few relevant conversations | Account coverage, stakeholder coverage and protected prospecting time | Rebuild the active pool and increase focused activity |
| Low reply rate | Account fit, signal, stakeholder, message, proof and channel | Improve relevance before adding volume |
| Low meeting conversion | Ask, timing, clarity and buyer commitment | Tighten the opening and reason to meet |
| Low qualification rate | Workflow pain, owner, consequence and timing | Refine the ICP and discovery standard |
| Qualified deals not advancing | Champion, stakeholder access, proof, buying process and dated next step | Resolve the missing buyer milestone or disqualify |

**Shorter version:**

> I separate coverage from conversion. I inspect whether I have enough of the right accounts and activity, then find the stage where conversion is breaking. I change the actual constraint—account fit, stakeholder, message, proof or follow-up—rather than responding with random volume. I also involve my manager or AD early, show the data and agree on one or two changes to inspect the next week.

### 101. How do you prioritize your day?

> I prioritize around customer commitments, pipeline quality and protected creation time.
>
> First, I handle anything where a customer or internal partner is waiting on me: a meeting, promised follow-up, technical answer or dated next step. Reliability compounds, and I do not want avoidable delay to become deal risk.
>
> Second, I protect a focused block for priority-account work. I keep a daily top-three queue: the three accounts or stakeholders where a researched action today has the highest expected value. That could be a new signal, a credible path into the buying group, or a live opportunity with one missing stakeholder or proof point.
>
> Third, I preserve consistent outbound and follow-up so active deals do not consume the entire day. At Airwallex, the discipline was to keep building and replenishing the book while advancing live accounts; the exact daily mix changed, but pipeline creation did not disappear because closing work became busy.
>
> I finish by closing the loop in CRM: what changed, who is involved, what is blocked, what proof is missing, who owns the next action and when it is due.
>
> I also maintain a monitor list and a do-not-touch-yet list. That prevents me from treating every attractive logo as equally ready for outreach.

**Daily priority stack:**

1. Customer and Account Director commitments with real deadlines.
2. The top three accounts or stakeholders where action today is most valuable.
3. Protected pipeline creation and appropriately spaced follow-up.
4. Meeting preparation and opportunity advancement.
5. CRM closure, next-step scheduling and learning capture.

**Shorter version:**

> I start with real customer and AD commitments, then work a daily top-three account queue based on fit, fresh signal and the next buyer milestone. I protect time for new pipeline even when live deals are busy, and I end by recording the blocker, proof gap, owner and dated next step. I also keep a monitor list and a do-not-touch-yet list so attention follows evidence rather than logo prestige.

### 102. How do you maintain follow-up and CRM discipline?

> I treat CRM as the shared decision record for the account, not as administration I do after the selling.
>
> After a meaningful interaction, I capture seven things: the account thesis, the buyer's language for the problem, the stakeholder map, what was validated, what remains uncertain, the current blocker and the dated next step with an owner.
>
> I separate buyer-confirmed facts from my inference. If the customer has confirmed a workflow or decision date, I record it as confirmed. If I believe the CFO may become a blocker but have not validated that, I label it as a hypothesis. That makes the opportunity easier for an Account Director or manager to inspect without inheriting my optimism as fact.
>
> Every real opportunity should have a customer-owned next step. I schedule the follow-up while the context is fresh, put the appropriate reminder in the system and update the record when the buyer changes the plan. For weaker accounts, I record the missing signal and a sensible revisit condition rather than creating endless generic reminders.
>
> My standard is simple: if another seller had to take over tomorrow, could they understand why the account matters, who matters, what has happened, what could kill the opportunity and what should happen next?

**Minimum account record:**

| Field | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Account thesis | Why this account and workflow? |
| Source and status | What is verified, inferred or stale? |
| Stakeholder map | Who owns, influences, evaluates, approves or blocks? |
| Buyer evidence | What has the customer actually confirmed or done? |
| Proof gap | What still has to become credible? |
| Blocker | What is currently preventing progress? |
| Owner and dated next step | Who will do what by when? |

**Shorter version:**

> I use CRM as a shared decision record. I capture the thesis, stakeholder map, buyer-confirmed facts, open assumptions, blocker, proof gap and dated next step with an owner. I separate fact from inference and record a revisit condition for accounts that are not ready. The test is whether an AD could inspect the account tomorrow and understand both the opportunity and the risk without needing the context from my head.

### 103. How do you handle rejection?

> I treat rejection as information without pretending it never affects me.
>
> My first step is to classify it. Is this the wrong account, the wrong stakeholder, weak timing, an unimportant problem, an unsupported message, insufficient proof, the wrong channel, or a genuine competitive decision?
>
> Then I route the account appropriately. Some should be disqualified. Some should be moved to nurture with a specific trigger to watch. Some justify a different stakeholder or a sharper point of view. What I avoid is converting every no into either self-doubt or blind persistence.
>
> At Airwallex, email and LinkedIn did not produce engagement from EU Holidays. I did not conclude that the account was permanently uninterested, but I also did not send an endless sequence. I strengthened the account thesis, prepared the stakeholder map, tailored deck and ROI model, and used a different channel by visiting the office and asking for the Head of Finance. The lesson was not that every rejection deserves more persistence; it was that strong fit plus a weak channel deserves a thoughtful reroute.
>
> I capture the reason, update the account hypothesis and keep my overall activity consistent. Rejection becomes useful when it improves the next decision.

**Rejection taxonomy:**

- **Wrong account:** Disqualify or deprioritize.
- **Wrong stakeholder:** Remap the buying group and change the entry point.
- **Wrong timing:** Nurture against a defined trigger or date.
- **Weak problem or value:** Improve the thesis or stop pursuing.
- **Missing proof:** Bring a relevant reference, business case or controlled validation step.
- **Wrong channel:** Reroute only when account fit and timing still justify the effort.
- **Competitive loss:** Capture why the buyer chose the alternative and update the playbook.

**Shorter version:**

> I classify rejection before reacting: account, stakeholder, timing, problem, proof, message, channel or competition. Then I disqualify, nurture or reroute deliberately. EU Holidays is a good example: LinkedIn and email did not work, but the account thesis remained strong, so I changed the channel and approached the Head of Finance in person with tailored preparation. I learn from the no without taking it personally or persisting blindly.

>
> I became too focused on the work directly in front of me: delivering for active customers, closing current opportunities, improving the product and keeping customers happy. >
> When churn hit, I paid for it. I had to rebuild pipeline from too close to zero instead of having a healthy bench of accounts, warm conversations and next-best prospects already moving. I also had two employees at the time, so it felt larger than a personal miss. I had allowed the commercial system supporting the team to become fragile.
>
> I owned that the issue was not market timing or churn itself. Churn and stalled deals are normal. The preventable mistake was assuming I could restart prospecting only when I needed it.
>

AI and automation made the work more efficient, but the real correction was behavioral—pipeline generation became a protected habit rather than something dependent on my mood or available time.
>
> The durable lesson is that a seller has to respect top-of-funnel creation even when delivery and live deals feel more urgent. Being busy is not the same as protecting the future book.

### 105. Tell me about a deal you lost.

> One important loss was a paid platform opportunity with Binance while I was at Boxo.
>
> Binance was evaluating whether to use Boxo's mini-app platform or build the capability internally. I had a contact who was engaged, asked detailed questions and spent time understanding our platform, partner ecosystem and implementation model. I interpreted that level of engagement as evidence that we had a serious buying process.
>
> My mistake was relying too heavily on that one contact. I did not reach and convince the true economic buyer, map how the internal build-versus-buy decision would actually be made, or force a clear comparison around engineering capacity, maintenance burden, speed and roadmap opportunity cost.
>
> In hindsight, the contact was partly using us to learn for the internal assessment. They were a valuable source of information, but I had mistaken engagement for commitment. Binance ultimately chose to build internally because it had the engineering resources to do so.
>
> I own the loss because I should have tested champion quality earlier. Could this person explain why the project had to happen now? Could they identify the economic and technical decision-makers? Would they bring us into those conversations? What evidence could genuinely change the build-versus-buy decision?
>
> Since then, I distinguish a curious contact from a real champion through behavior. A real champion shares the internal map, helps us reach the required stakeholders, corrects the business case and spends credibility moving the decision forward. If those behaviors are absent, the conversation may still be useful discovery, but I do not treat it as committed pipeline.

**What changed in my qualification:**

1. Test economic-buyer access earlier.
2. Map the technical and commercial decision process, not only product interest.
3. Define the buyer's build-versus-buy criteria explicitly.
4. Ask what evidence could actually change the decision.
5. Separate information-gathering engagement from internal advocacy.

**Shorter version:**

> I lost a Boxo platform opportunity with Binance to internal build. I had a highly engaged contact and mistook detailed interest for buying commitment. I did not reach the real economic buyer or make the full build-versus-buy decision explicit enough. Binance had the engineering resources and chose to build. Since then, I test champion quality through action: stakeholder access, internal context, co-authorship of the case and willingness to spend credibility—not responsiveness alone.

### 107. What is your biggest weakness?

> One weakness I have been actively working on is answering with too much context before giving the literal answer.
>
> I naturally see the connected system around a question, and that is useful in account strategy. But in an interview or executive conversation, it can cause me to explain the background before answering the noun the person actually asked for. That makes the listener work harder and can reduce confidence even when the underlying answer is strong.
>
> I received a clear reminder of that in my recent hiring-leader conversation. Several times, the direct answer arrived after the supporting context instead of before it.
>
> I now use a simple discipline: answer the question in the first sentence, give one proof point, then stop and let the other person pull for detail. For numbers, I use one metric per sentence and state its basis. For uncertain facts, I caveat them rather than filling the silence with extra explanation.
>
> I still want to preserve the curiosity and depth that help me sell complex products. The change is sequencing: direct answer first, useful context second.

**Correction mechanism:**

`Literal answer → one proof point → stop or invite the next question`

**Shorter version:**

> I can over-explain before answering directly. That depth helps in account research, but it can make executive communication harder to follow. I now answer the question in the first sentence, add one proof point and stop. I also use one metric per sentence and caveat uncertainty explicitly. The goal is to keep the substance while making it easier to consume.

### 110. How do you handle ambiguity?

> I handle ambiguity by creating a small evidence loop rather than waiting for perfect instructions or pretending I already know the answer.
>
> I start by defining the outcome, then separate what is known from what is assumed. I gather the strongest available evidence, choose the smallest useful test and decide in advance what would change the plan. After the test, I preserve what I learned so the next decision begins from a higher baseline.
>
> Airwallex Singapore is a good example. I joined as the second outbound hire, so there was not a complete local outbound playbook. I used existing UK call evidence to form an early hypothesis that travel could be a productive vertical, then localized it through Singapore travel-fair conversations, company brochures, destination and corridor research, and direct buyer conversations. When LinkedIn and email did not open EU Holidays, I did not treat the original channel plan as sacred. I strengthened the point of view and tested a more direct office approach.
>
> That motion eventually became more structured: travel and retail ICP rules, account-priority criteria, stakeholder hypotheses, tailored messaging and the 51-page FAQ. The important pattern was hypothesis, evidence, test, review and codification.
>
> At OpenAI, I would use the same discipline while respecting that I must first learn the team's definitions and constraints. Ambiguity is permission to investigate and own the next step; it is not permission to invent facts or bypass the people who hold context.

**Ambiguity loop:**

1. Define the outcome.
2. Separate facts, assumptions and unknowns.
3. Gather the best available customer and internal evidence.
4. Run the smallest useful test.
5. Review what changed and decide whether to continue, revise or stop.
6. Capture the learning in the account plan or playbook.

**Shorter version:**

> I create a small evidence loop: outcome, facts and assumptions, smallest test, review and codification. At Airwallex Singapore, I used UK call evidence to hypothesize that travel could work, localized it through travel-fair and account research, changed channel when digital outreach failed, and turned the learning into a repeatable local motion. I move quickly in ambiguity, but I keep assumptions visible and update the plan when the evidence changes.

### 111. How do you use AI without outsourcing sales judgment?

> I use AI to increase the quality and speed of preparation, not to decide what is true or send on my behalf without review.
>
> It is useful for aggregating account context, searching prior notes, summarizing calls, mapping possible stakeholders, surfacing signals, comparing similar accounts, drafting a first account thesis and reminding me about open follow-ups. That reduces blank-page work and gives me more time for actual buyer conversations.
>
> But I keep four responsibilities human-owned.
>
> First is truth. I preserve source links, dates and confidence, and I verify consequential claims before using them with a buyer or writing them into CRM.
>
> Second is relevance. AI can surface ten facts; I have to decide which one matters to this stakeholder and whether it creates a legitimate reason to speak.
>
> Third is communication. I review and rewrite the message so it sounds like me, respects the buyer and does not turn a common public signal into automated spam.
>
> Fourth is commercial judgment. I decide account priority, qualification, stakeholder sequencing, negotiation boundaries and whether the evidence supports advancing the opportunity.
>
> My own GTM work reinforced this. When building account-intelligence systems, the useful output was not "the AI sent more emails." It was a cleaner market map, source-backed evidence, confidence labels, CRM-safe routing and a stronger recommendation for a human seller to review. I judge the workflow by pipeline quality, preparation time and deal movement—not output volume.

**Preparation versus judgment:**

| AI can assist | Seth remains accountable |
|---|---|
| Research aggregation and retrieval | Whether the claim is true and current |
| Signal and stakeholder suggestions | Whether the signal is relevant and messageable |
| Call summaries and follow-up drafts | Whether the summary and next step match the conversation |
| Account-thesis and message drafts | Final point of view, language and send decision |
| CRM-update suggestions | High-impact commercial fields and opportunity status |
| Pattern detection across accounts | Qualification, prioritization and commercial decisions |

**Shorter version:**

> I use AI for research, retrieval, call synthesis, drafts and workflow memory, but I keep truth, relevance, communication and commercial decisions human-owned. I preserve sources and confidence, verify consequential claims, rewrite every buyer-facing message and review any CRM update. The test is whether AI improves judgment density, pipeline quality or preparation—not whether it produces more activity.
