## VI. Champion Risk And Account Director Partnership

### 64. What do you do when a champion will not introduce other stakeholders?

> I would treat that as a diagnosis before treating it as an objection. The champion may not yet see enough value, may lack influence, may be protecting their own credibility or may know that another stakeholder is politically difficult.
>
> I would ask directly but constructively: “What would make it difficult to bring Product or the economic buyer into the conversation?” Then I would reduce the risk of the introduction. I might give them a short forwardable business case, propose a tightly scoped agenda and explain exactly why that stakeholder is needed—for example, Product to validate the workflow or Finance to validate the value case.
>
> Then I would test behavior. Will the contact correct the case, explain the decision path, share the stakeholder's concerns or agree on a route to access? If they repeatedly say, “Let me socialize it,” but nothing happens, I would stop calling them a champion. I might continue gathering useful account intelligence or pursue another legitimate path into the account, but I would downgrade or disqualify the opportunity until there is real stakeholder access.
>
> Binance taught me that lesson. I had an engaged contact who asked detailed questions, but I mistook information gathering for internal sponsorship. I never established enough access to the economic buyer, and Binance ultimately chose to build internally. Since then, I have treated willingness and ability to widen the conversation as evidence—not assumed it from enthusiasm.

**My diagnostic sequence:**

1. **Value:** Does the contact believe the problem is important enough to spend internal credibility on?
2. **Power:** Can they reach the people who approve, block or implement the decision?
3. **Safety:** What personal, political or operational risk makes the introduction uncomfortable?
4. **Enablement:** What concise case, proof or agenda would make the introduction safer?
5. **Behavior:** Do they take a concrete step, or only promise to “socialize” material?
6. **Qualification:** If access remains unavailable, downgrade the deal rather than carrying false confidence.

**Simple language for requesting access:**

> Based on what we have learned, the commercial case seems worth exploring, but Product would need to validate whether this fits the roadmap. What would make it difficult to include them? If it helps, I can send a three-point agenda and keep the conversation focused on that decision.

**Raw-source basis:** Seth's Binance lost-deal account in `OpenAI-SDR-comprehensive-answer-expansion-bank.md` confirms that Binance chose internal build, Seth did not reach or convince the economic buyer and the contact behaved more like an information-gathering contact than a committed champion. Brian LaManna's captured post (`#318000`, lines 30–54) supports the red flag that a contact who never loops in others may not be a champion. Those methodology sources do not establish the Binance facts.

### 65. How do you communicate differently with executives, managers, technical evaluators and users?

> I keep one business case, but translate it into the decision each stakeholder owns.
>
> With an executive, I lead with the company goal, why now, the economic or strategic upside and the risk of acting or not acting. With a manager or workflow owner, I get more concrete about the metric, process change, resourcing and adoption. With a technical evaluator, I focus on architecture, integration, data boundaries, failure modes, support ownership and what must be proven. With a user, I stay close to the actual workflow: what becomes easier, what remains under their control and what happens when the system is wrong.
>
> At UNA, the value spine was faster, repeatable service expansion. The CEO heard platform growth and monetization. The Head of Partnerships heard faster partner launches. Product heard a coherent embedded experience. Engineering heard a credible integration model and a clear build-versus-buy comparison. Legal and risk heard explicit data and responsibility boundaries.
>
> The message changes, but the facts should not. If every stakeholder hears a different promise, the seller has created confusion rather than alignment.

**Stakeholder translation:**

| Stakeholder | Lead with | Evidence they need |
|---|---|---|
| Executive or economic buyer | Goal, why now, investment, strategic upside and risk | Business case, credible path and decision trade-off |
| Manager or workflow owner | KPI, process, capacity, adoption and ownership | Current-to-target workflow and implementation plan |
| Technical evaluator | Feasibility, architecture, integrations, controls and failure handling | Technical validation, boundaries and proof criteria |
| Legal, compliance or risk | Data, responsibility, consent, auditability and liability | Clear ownership, controls and support path |
| User or operator | Daily steps, time saved, exceptions, review and escalation | Representative workflow and usable handoff |

**Remember it as:**

> Executive: goal and risk. Manager: metric and process. Technical evaluator: feasibility and control. User: workflow and trust.

**Raw-source basis:** The UNA stakeholder roles and value case are Seth-confirmed and reconciled in the final pack's Boxo section. QMD-retrieved `High-Signal Enterprise Sales` (`#643f03`, lines 140–177) and `AI-Native Account Intelligence` (`#5536ad`, lines 127–179) support stakeholder-level language and distinct proof requirements. `Founder-Led Enterprise Sales Playbooks` (`#da523b`, lines 104–158) supports matching the message to developer, product, executive, procurement and finance audiences.

### 66. What does good partnership with an Account Director look like?

> Good AD partnership means making the Account Director's judgment better at every stage, not simply filling their calendar.
>
> Before outreach, I would align on the account thesis: why the account matters, the likely workflow, relevant signals, stakeholder hypothesis, existing relationships and what would make the opportunity worth deeper investment. During development, I would keep the state legible—what the buyer has confirmed, what remains an assumption, who is engaged, what proof is missing, what could kill the deal and what the next meeting must accomplish.
>
> I would bring the AD in when their seniority, relationship or commercial judgment can materially advance the account—for example, executive discovery, an important stakeholder meeting or a discussion where scope and commercial strategy are becoming real. I would brief them properly rather than make them rediscover the account live.
>
> Once the AD joins, I would respect that they own the formal close. I would remain useful through account research, stakeholder development, discovery follow-up and context preservation, while agreeing explicitly on who communicates what. The goal is one account team with one customer story, not two sellers running parallel motions.

**The partnership loop:**

1. **Align:** Account tier, whitespace, point of view, target stakeholders and definition of a worthwhile opportunity.
2. **Develop:** Validate the workflow, priority, owner, buying group, proof gaps and risks.
3. **Brief:** Separate confirmed evidence from assumptions and define the purpose of the AD's involvement.
4. **Team:** Agree who leads each part of the next conversation and which buyer decision it should unlock.
5. **Continue:** Preserve context, open missing threads and close action items without confusing commercial ownership.
6. **Review:** Feed buyer evidence and loss patterns back into territory and account strategy.

**Simple example:**

> If I were handing EU Holidays to an AD, I would not say, “I booked the Head of Finance.” I would say: the account is a high-priority outbound-tour operator; payment reliability was around 80% against a desired state above 99%; the Head of Finance is the champion; Operations and Commercial are relevant; the CEO is the final decision-maker; FX is the controlled initial wedge; and the next meeting must validate volume, implementation ownership and the proof plan. The main risk is trust in moving volume away from familiar banks.

**Raw-source basis:** The official JD says the BDR partners with ADs on account strategy, multithreading and deal progression. The recruiter described strategic work on large, multi-threaded accounts. The July 17 hiring-leader evidence states that Strategic BDRs source and develop opportunities while the AD owns the final close, and that later involvement may vary by account and AD relationship. The EU Holidays facts come from Seth's reconciled Airwallex case. QMD's buyer-evidence framework shaped the briefing structure but does not establish OpenAI's internal handoff policy.

> [!warning] OpenAI process boundary
> This is Seth's proposed partnership model, not a claim about a fixed internal OpenAI workflow. Exact meeting ownership, acceptance criteria and post-handoff involvement should be aligned with the specific AD and manager.

### 67. What makes an opportunity AD-ready?

> An opportunity is AD-ready when the AD can understand the account, the buyer's reason to act and the next decision without having to repeat the first discovery call.
>
> I would hand over eight things: why this account, why now, the workflow and current-state problem, the business consequence, the owner or champion, the stakeholder and power map, the proof and process still required, and the objective for the next meeting. I would also identify what could kill the opportunity and separate confirmed facts from my hypotheses.
>
> AD-ready does not mean every MEDDICC field is complete. Early in a strategic deal, some information will remain unknown. It means there is enough buyer evidence to justify the AD's attention and a clear plan to resolve the important unknowns.
>
> A calendar invite with an interested contact is not a handoff. A customer-validated problem, credible owner, stakeholder path and decision-oriented next step is.

**AD-ready opportunity packet:**

| Field | What the AD should receive |
|---|---|
| Why this account | Fit, potential value and strategic relevance |
| Why now | Trigger, initiative, deadline or consequence |
| Workflow | Current process, friction and desired state |
| Business impact | Metric, risk, cost of inaction or strategic outcome |
| People | Initial owner, champion strength, economic-buyer path and wider buying group |
| Proof | What the buyer must believe and what evidence is missing |
| Process and risk | Decision path, technical/risk/procurement questions and possible disqualifiers |
| Next meeting | Named objective, participants, owner, date and intended buyer decision |

**EU Holidays handoff example:**

> EU Holidays is a priority Singapore outbound-tour operator. The validated workflow is overseas supplier and guide payments, where reliability was around 80% and month-end reconciliation required five people for roughly two days. The Head of Finance is the champion; Operations and Commercial are relevant; the CEO is the final decision-maker. FX is the controlled first wedge. The next meeting should confirm volume, implementation ownership and how reliability will be evaluated. The primary risk is confidence in moving volume away from existing banks.

### 68. Where should the BDR retain ownership after the AD joins?

> I would retain ownership of the work that created the opportunity, while making the AD's commercial ownership unmistakable.
>
> That usually means continuing to refresh account intelligence, develop missing stakeholder threads, preserve discovery context, coordinate agreed follow-ups and keep the CRM state current. If I have a trusted relationship with the champion or another stakeholder, I would not disappear and waste that trust. I would use it in support of the shared account plan.
>
> I would not run a parallel sales process, make independent commercial commitments or surprise the AD with outreach into sensitive stakeholders. Before and after each meeting, I would agree on roles: who leads, who listens for which signal, who follows up and what buyer action counts as progress.
>
> The exact boundary should vary by account and by the AD's working style. The principle is that the BDR retains useful context and momentum; the AD retains clear ownership of the formal close.

**A clean division of labor:**

| BDR continues to own or support | AD owns or directs |
|---|---|
| Account signals and research refresh | Overall commercial strategy and formal close |
| Missing stakeholder identification and approved outreach | Executive and commercial sequencing |
| Discovery notes, buyer evidence and proof-gap tracking | Scope, pricing, negotiation and forecast judgment |
| Agreed follow-up and next-step coordination | Commercial commitments and final decision path |
| CRM context and handoff integrity | Account-team roles and customer communication strategy |

**Simple account-team cadence:**

> Before the next meeting: agree on the decision to unlock and divide roles. After the meeting: compare what the buyer confirmed, update the stakeholder and proof map, assign actions and decide whether the account actually advanced.

**Raw-source basis:** The July 17 hiring-leader evidence says Strategic BDRs source and develop opportunities but do not own the final close, and may remain involved in later calls depending on the account and AD relationship. The recruiter described meaningful account ownership beyond meeting booking. The official JD covers account strategy, multithreading and deal progression. This answer deliberately avoids claiming a fixed OpenAI RACI.

> [!warning] OpenAI process boundary
> Verify actual post-acceptance responsibilities with the interviewer. “Retain ownership” means remain accountable for useful upstream work and account context, not compete with the AD for control of the deal.

## VII. Demo, Proof, Objections And Competition

### 69. How do you design a buyer-specific demo?

> I start by asking what the buyer needs to believe, not by choosing which features I want to show.
>
> Before the demo, I confirm the workflow, audience, current friction and two or three proof questions. Then I build the shortest possible story around a representative case: the trigger, the relevant context, the useful output or action, the exception path and the handoff into the buyer's real workflow. I label anything mocked and avoid presenting prototype behavior as production readiness.
>
> During the demo, I keep returning to the buyer's criteria and watch the room. If the audience disengages or the conversation exposes a more important unknown, I stop the feature tour and return to discovery.
>
> I end by asking what they now believe, what remains unproven and whether the evidence justifies the next step. The demo is successful when it produces a shared conclusion, not when I finish every screen.

**Demo design sequence:**

1. **Decision:** What buyer decision should the demo make easier?
2. **Beliefs:** What must each attendee believe for the workflow to be credible?
3. **Case:** Which representative scenario will make the proof close to their environment?
4. **Happy path:** Show only the steps required to prove the core outcome.
5. **Failure path:** Show the relevant exception, control or human handoff.
6. **Boundary:** Mark mocks, assumptions and production gaps explicitly.
7. **Conclusion:** Ask what was proven, what was not and what should happen next.

**Simple Sunder example:**

> For a law-firm buyer, I would not demonstrate a generic legal chatbot. I would use a representative or redacted claimant file and show the narrow workflow the buyer cared about: organize the source documents, identify missing or inconsistent information, produce a source-linked review pack and route uncertain items to a person. Lawyers would retain editing, legal judgment and sign-off. The point would be to prove a safer evidence-preparation workflow, not autonomous legal advice.

**Raw-source basis:** The Sunder/Hoh workflow, source-linked outputs and lawyer-review boundary are documented in the comprehensive bank. The June 29 sales-coaching source says demos should begin with buyer motivation, problem and proof, remain brief and return to discovery when attention drops; it is coaching, not official OpenAI sales policy. QMD-retrieved `High-Signal Enterprise Sales` (`#643f03`, lines 89–117) supports asking the buyer what the product must prove and avoiding premature group demos. `Founder-Led Enterprise Sales Playbooks` (`#da523b`, lines 77–88) supports use-case demos rather than product tours.

### 70. How do you define proof criteria before a demo or pilot?

> I define proof as a decision contract before the work starts.
>
> The buyer and seller should agree on the workflow, current baseline, target outcome, representative cases, guardrails, owner, duration and the decision that follows. I also define what is out of scope and what the proof cannot establish. For an AI workflow, an efficiency metric should have a quality or risk guardrail so we do not create a false improvement by making the output faster but worse.
>
> Most importantly, the customer should co-author the criteria. If I wait until after the result and then choose the metric that looks best, that is a demonstration, not a credible proof.

**Proof charter:**

| Element | Question to answer |
|---|---|
| Workflow | Which bounded process or use case are we testing? |
| Baseline | How does it perform today? |
| Target | What measurable change would support the decision? |
| Representative cases | Which normal, edge and failure cases must be included? |
| Guardrails | What quality, risk or customer outcome must not deteriorate? |
| Owners and inputs | Who supplies data, users, access, review and support? |
| Duration and checkpoints | When will we inspect progress and end the test? |
| Decision | What specific go, iterate or stop decision follows? |
| Boundary | What does this proof not establish about production or scale? |

**EU Holidays example:**

> For EU Holidays, the safe first proof was controlled FX volume rather than asking them to move everything away from familiar banks. The current-state concerns were approximately 80% payment reliability and a five-person, two-day month-end reconciliation process with recurring double-counting errors. The desired states were above 99% reliability and a largely automated process with Finance performing a final review. A credible proof plan would define the initial corridors and volume, how successful arrival and reconciliation would be measured, who reviewed exceptions and what result justified expansion.

**Raw-source basis:** Seth confirmed the EU Holidays current-state and desired-state metrics, the controlled-volume wedge and the trust barrier around existing banks. QMD-retrieved `High-Signal Enterprise Sales` (`#643f03`, lines 89–138) supports buyer-defined proof; the customer-verifiable exit-criteria capture (`#c0fda5`, lines 23–55) supports co-created problem and business-case artifacts. The enterprise PoV section of the comprehensive bank supplies the baseline, guardrail and explicit decision structure.

### 71. How do you use a pilot or controlled rollout without creating pilot purgatory?

> A pilot should answer a decision question, have mutual commitment and end in a defined decision.
>
> Before it starts, I want a real workflow owner, representative users or data, buyer-defined success criteria, a time boundary and clarity on what happens after a positive, mixed or negative result. I also want the production dependencies visible early—security, integrations, implementation ownership, change management and commercial scope—so success does not lead to another undefined evaluation.
>
> During the pilot, I run checkpoints against the agreed criteria and track where failures come from. I do not keep adding features to rescue an unclear hypothesis. At the end, the options are go, iterate with a specific new hypothesis or stop.
>
> At EU Holidays, the controlled rollout was the right risk-reduction mechanism: land with a limited FX workflow, prove reliability in real use and expand only after the first wedge earned trust. That is different from an open-ended free trial where nobody has agreed what decision will follow.

**Anti-purgatory controls:**

1. A named business owner and active user group.
2. One falsifiable value or risk hypothesis.
3. A baseline, target and guardrail.
4. Customer commitments for data, access, review and stakeholder time.
5. A fixed duration with scheduled checkpoints.
6. Production and commercial dependencies identified before the end.
7. A pre-agreed `go`, `iterate` or `stop` decision meeting.
8. No extension without a specific unresolved hypothesis and reciprocal commitment.

**Simple line:**

> A pilot proves a criterion. It should not become a polite waiting room between interest and a decision.

**Raw-source basis:** The verified EU Holidays case establishes a controlled FX wedge followed by expansion after reliability and trust were demonstrated. QMD-retrieved `Founder-Led Enterprise Sales Playbooks` (`#da523b`, lines 90–102) supports the distinction that trials prove product function while scoped pilots and commitments test seriousness. The comprehensive bank's PoV material supports buyer-defined criteria, representative users, a baseline and an explicit go/iterate/stop decision.

### 72. What do you do if the pilot misses its target?

> I do not move the goalposts or manufacture a success story. I freeze the agreed criteria, show the evidence and diagnose which layer failed.
>
> I would separate the business hypothesis from the implementation. Was the use case not valuable enough? Was the baseline wrong? Was the data unrepresentative? Did the model or workflow fail? Was an integration unreliable? Did users not adopt it? Or was the measurement itself poor?
>
> Then I would recommend one of three outcomes. Stop if the value hypothesis is wrong or the constraint is fundamental. Iterate if there is a specific, testable fix inside the agreed scope. Rescope only if the evidence points to a narrower or prerequisite workflow—and make clear that this is a new hypothesis, not the original pilot passing.
>
> In the Hoh Law pilot, every point where a reviewer had to stop was treated as diagnostic evidence. We classified whether the problem came from extraction, a missing schema field, validation, the review experience or escalation. We then converted the issue into a rule, field, review state or test case and reran it. I would use the same discipline if the overall target were missed: diagnose honestly, learn quickly and protect the customer's confidence with a clear recommendation.

**Failure diagnosis:**

| Layer | Diagnostic question | Possible action |
|---|---|---|
| Business hypothesis | Does improving this workflow create enough value? | Stop or choose a more valuable workflow |
| Baseline and metric | Did we measure the right current state and outcome? | Correct the measurement and rerun only if justified |
| Scope and cases | Was the test representative and bounded? | Narrow or rebuild the case set |
| Data or knowledge | Were inputs complete, current and usable? | Fix the prerequisite data problem |
| Model or workflow | Where did quality, tool use or state handling fail? | Update logic, prompts, schemas, evals or guardrails |
| Integration and operations | Did latency, reliability or system access undermine the result? | Repair the operational dependency |
| Adoption | Did real users trust and use the workflow correctly? | Fix training, workflow fit, ownership or change design |

**Decision language:**

> The original target was X, and we achieved Y. The evidence suggests the main constraint was Z. My recommendation is to stop, iterate once against this specific hypothesis or rescope to this prerequisite workflow. I would not present the original target as met.

**Raw-source basis:** The Hoh Law/Sunder evidence confirms a roughly three-month paid pilot, selected users, real or redacted file feedback, phased rollout and classification of review stops into schema, extraction, validation, UX or escalation issues. The comprehensive bank explicitly warns that the pilot did not prove broad PMF, standardized implementation, perfect accuracy or autonomous legal work. Its PoV section supplies the `stop`, `iterate` or `rescope` decision logic.

### 73. How do you handle build versus buy?

> I begin by accepting that internal build may be the correct answer. That makes the conversation more credible and lets us compare the real options rather than defend a vendor by default.
>
> I would compare six things: strategic control, speed to value, internal talent and capacity, total maintenance burden, integration and governance requirements, and opportunity cost. I would also consider coexistence—a customer may keep proprietary control of the differentiating layer while buying infrastructure or capabilities that are expensive to recreate and maintain.
>
> At UNA, Engineering could reasonably build individual integrations. The stronger comparison was whether UNA wanted to hire and maintain enough capacity to launch many merchants and categories, support the integration layer and accept the roadmap work that would be displaced. Boxo offered a repeatable path. The initial eSIM scope was approximately USD 200K ARR, later expanded into e-commerce and additional merchants, and UNA achieved its contractual target of approximately 100 merchant launches in the first year.
>
> Binance is the useful contrast. It had the engineering resources and chose internal build. My mistake was not proving early enough whether there was a business reason strong enough to overcome that preference or reaching the economic buyer. The lesson is that build-versus-buy should qualify the opportunity, not merely become an objection handled at the end.

**Build-versus-buy comparison:**

| Dimension | Build may be stronger when | Buy or coexist may be stronger when |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic control | The capability is genuinely differentiating | The capability is enabling infrastructure |
| Speed | The team can deliver within the business deadline | Hiring and development would delay the initiative |
| Talent and capacity | The expertise and roadmap capacity already exist | Scarce engineers would be pulled from higher-value work |
| Maintenance | The company wants to own upgrades, support and edge cases | Ongoing maintenance across many integrations is burdensome |
| Integration and governance | Internal architecture requires deep custom control | A vendor can meet the required controls and integration model |
| Economics | Long-term internal ownership is lower-cost at realistic scale | Total cost of hiring, delay, maintenance and risk favors buying |
| Optionality | A proprietary system creates durable advantage | Coexistence preserves control while accelerating non-core layers |

**Discovery questions:**

1. What part of this capability is genuinely proprietary or differentiating?
2. What is the deadline, and what happens if internal delivery slips?
3. Which engineers would build and maintain it, and what would they stop doing?
4. What integrations, controls, support and edge cases must be owned for years?
5. What evidence would justify buy, build or coexist?
6. Who owns the economic trade-off—not only the technical preference?

**Raw-source basis:** Seth confirmed that build-versus-buy, not price, was UNA's principal commercial comparison; UNA's initial and expanded ARR, use-case sequence and approximately 100-merchant achieved target are reconciled in the final pack. Seth also confirmed that Binance chose internal build because it had the engineering resources and that he failed to reach or convince the real economic buyer. QMD methodology supports evaluating maintenance, opportunity cost and proprietary context, but does not establish either account's facts.

> [!warning] Truth boundary
> Do not imply that internal build is irrational, introduce an unverified UNA pricing objection or invent Binance commercial details. The correct conclusion can be buy, build or coexist.
