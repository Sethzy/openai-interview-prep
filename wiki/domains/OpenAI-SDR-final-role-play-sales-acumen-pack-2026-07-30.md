# OpenAI SDR Final Role-Play Sales Acumen Pack

## Purpose

This is the focused preparation pack for the final-final interview. The working assumption is that the interviewer will test whether Seth can already think and operate like an OpenAI Strategic BDR, rather than asking only for retrospective career stories.

This pack is **sales acumen only**. It has two jobs:

1. Prepare Seth to role-play territory design, outbound prospecting, campaign strategy, stakeholder entry, and regional judgment at OpenAI.
2. Make Seth fluent enough in fintech infrastructure to explain his Airwallex experience credibly to an interviewer with Stripe experience, culminating in a canonical TruTrip competitive-win story.

It does not replace [[OpenAI-SDR-final-sales-acumen-interview-pack]]. That document remains the evidence bank. This document is the narrow final-round rehearsal surface.

## How To Answer In The Interview

Use the same four-part structure for most questions:

1. **Answer the literal question immediately.**
2. **Give the framework or commercial judgment.**
3. **Prove it with one Airwallex, Boxo, or Salescraft example.**
4. **Translate it into what Seth would do at OpenAI.**

Target 45–90 seconds unless the interviewer asks Seth to role-play the full workflow.

## Part I — OpenAI Role-Play: Core Questions

These are the six highest-priority questions Seth explicitly expects.

### 1. Which markets have you sold into, and what did each market teach you?

> I would separate my experience by the type of ownership I had.
>
> At Airwallex, Singapore was my formal territory. I was a full-cycle, self-sourcing AE, so I owned everything from account selection and outbound through discovery, commercial negotiation, and close.
>
> At Boxo, my center of gravity was Southeast Asia, with selected strategic accounts elsewhere in APAC. I worked with or qualified companies including Zalo and MoMo in Vietnam, GCash and Maya in the Philippines, DANA in Indonesia, Touch 'n Go in Malaysia, TrueMoney in Thailand, Grab in Singapore, and Paytm in India. I would not claim that I owned every country as a standalone territory—the depth of my involvement varied by account—but the role gave me direct exposure to how differently these markets buy.
>
> Singapore was the most commercially forward. It had clearer buyer maps, stronger access to regional decision-makers, and companies that were generally more comfortable evaluating new technology.
>
> Vietnam and India were more engineering-led. With companies such as Zalo and Paytm, engineering and product were first-class stakeholders. Commercial interest was not enough; we had to establish technical credibility and demonstrate how the product would integrate with what they were already building. The main risk there was build vs buy. It was likely they were building an internal version of the integrations, and it was a question of whether we could convince them our solution would help them prioritise or accelerate their product roadmap.
>
> The Philippines and Indonesia were more trust-driven. Relationships, local credibility, and confidence in implementation mattered enormously. However, they were also most likely to lack technical resources. One of my most important qualification questions was whether the customer had sufficient internal technical resources to execute. A project could have a compelling use case and make commercial sense, but still be unready because nobody internally could own the integration.
>
> That also made me more conservative when forecasting opportunities in those markets. I would not treat enthusiasm or a recognizable logo as sufficient evidence. Before forecasting confidently, I would want to see a credible champion, an identified technical owner, committed implementation resources, and a concrete path to the next technical milestone.
>
> Thailand was surprisingly forward-looking when the use case was practical and supported by strong infrastructure. PromptPay, for example, launched ahead of many comparable real-time payment systems in Southeast Asia. That taught me not to judge market readiness purely by company size or conventional perceptions—some markets can move extremely quickly when the value is tangible and the supporting infrastructure already exists. However, just like in Vietnam and Indonesia, language could be a barrier.
>
> The broader lesson was that APAC is a collection of distinct markets. The same product may travel, but the buyer, initial wedge, required proof, technical readiness, and route to trust all change.
>
> At OpenAI, I would not assume that we are entering every market after the category has already matured. In parts of APAC, we will be recruiting the first innovators: customers willing to define what AI-enabled work looks like in their industry and become the reference that gives the next group confidence to adopt.
>
> My job would be to identify those genuine innovators, distinguish curiosity from readiness, and help build the local proof that allows the market to compound.

### 2. What is your outbound prospecting approach?

> If I joined OpenAI tomorrow, I would start with account strategy rather than message volume.
>
> First, I would align with the Account Director on the assigned accounts, existing relationships, active opportunities, previous conversations, and any approved internal product-usage signals. I would own the prospecting execution, but build it around a shared account strategy.
>
> I would then organize the account universe using five layers.

| Layer              | Purpose                                                                                     |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Industry           | Groups accounts with similar workflows, regulations, proof requirements, and buyer language |
| Company type       | Distinguishes traditional enterprises from digital natives                                  |
| Workflow           | Identifies the actual work OpenAI could improve                                             |
| Observable signals | Establishes why the account may be worth approaching now                                    |
| Account tier       | Determines how much research and human attention the account receives                       |

#### Organize The Research By Industry

> I would group account research by industry because that is where knowledge becomes reusable. Banks share certain workflows, regulations, stakeholder structures, and objections. Retailers share different customer-service and merchandising problems. Technology companies share engineering and product-development priorities.
>
> Within each industry, I would divide accounts into traditional enterprises and digital natives.

|                         | Traditional financial institutions                                                         | Digital-native fintechs                                                |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Examples                | Banks and insurers                                                                         | Wallets, payment platforms, neobanks and fintech products              |
| Starting condition      | Established systems, large operations and greater governance complexity                    | API-first products, larger technical teams and faster experimentation  |
| Likely OpenAI wedges    | ChatGPT Enterprise, customer operations, voice, bounded agents and internal Codex adoption | API, Codex, product AI, KYC or fraud workflows and customer agents     |
| Likely entry buyers     | CIO, transformation, operations, customer experience and technology                        | Founder, CTO, VP Engineering, Head of AI and product                   |
| Main qualification risk | Implementation complexity and organizational readiness                                     | Technical differentiation, economics, scalability and build-versus-buy |
|                         |                                                                                            |                                                                        |

> Industry organizes the research. Company type shapes the sales motion. The workflow determines the product hypothesis.

#### Build Separate Routes For The Two Company Types

|                       | Traditional enterprises                                                                                                   | Digital natives                                                         |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Initial question      | Where can AI improve employee work or a high-value operating workflow?                                                    | Where can OpenAI improve the product or increase engineering velocity?  |
| Likely first surfaces | ChatGPT Enterprise, Codex, workspace agents. <br><br>If more mature, sell them ,voice or other support governed workflows | Codex SDK or app server. API if they are building an ai native product. |
| Likely stakeholders   | CIO, CTO, transformation, business-unit and operations leaders                                                            | Founder, CTO, VP Engineering, Head of AI and product                    |
| Primary obstacle      | Implementation readiness and organizational change                                                                        | Quality, latency, economics, control and technical differentiation      |
| Expansion path        | Employee adoption → bounded workflow → wider operating deployment                                                         | Engineering or product wedge → greater usage → more teams and workloads |

The product remains a hypothesis at this stage.

> I would not approach the customer with the entire OpenAI catalogue. I would enter with a view on one important workflow and determine which product surface best supports it.

#### Find Observable Signals Of Readiness

> Fit tells me whether an account could become valuable. Signals tell me whether there is a reason to approach it now. Discovery determines whether the customer can actually move.

I would focus on four easily discoverable signal categories:

| Signal | Where I would find it | What it indicates |
|---|---|---|
| Existing adoption | Approved OpenAI usage data, CRM history, inbound interest and previous conversations | The account has already moved beyond abstract awareness |
| AI or engineering hiring | Company careers page and LinkedIn Jobs | The company is allocating people and potentially building execution capacity |
| Public AI commitment | Newsroom, annual reports, earnings transcripts and executive posts | AI has senior visibility and potentially budget |
| Product or operating trigger | Product announcements, engineering blogs, GitHub, funding, expansion or rising operating complexity | A specific workflow may have become more urgent |

I would look for multiple signals reinforcing the same workflow hypothesis.

> Hiring engineers alone does not automatically create a Codex opportunity. But hiring a developer-productivity leader, expanding the engineering organization and publicly prioritizing software-delivery speed creates a coherent Codex hypothesis.
>
> One signal earns further research. Two or more aligned signals can justify active outreach. Discovery confirms readiness.

#### Score Accounts Using Observable Evidence

The account-scoring rubric should not pretend that public research can reveal everything.

| Dimension            | Question                                                                               | How I would research it                                                                                                                     |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Strategic fit        | Is this an account OpenAI and the Account Director want to win?                        | Territory plan, CRM, prior account activity, existing relationships and direct AD alignment                                                 |
| Account potential    | Is the organization large or strategically important enough to justify focused effort? | Revenue, funding, employee count, engineering headcount, customer scale, markets served and public company filings                          |
| Workflow evidence    | Is there visible evidence of an important workflow OpenAI could improve?               | Product pages, support documentation, job descriptions, annual reports, customer reviews, engineering blogs and operating metrics           |
| Timing               | What changed that makes the conversation relevant now?                                 | Hiring, funding, executive changes, product launches, AI announcements, expansion and public transformation initiatives                     |
| Observable ownership | Can I identify a likely business or technical owner?                                   | Company leadership pages, LinkedIn Sales Navigator, job titles, conference speakers, interviews and press releases                          |
| Technical capacity   | Is there preliminary evidence that the company can build or integrate technology?      | Engineering headcount, AI hiring, technical blog, GitHub activity, cloud partnerships, API-first products and previous AI launches          |
| Expansion potential  | Could the initial workflow plausibly expand into other teams or products?              | Number of business units, employee and developer scale, geographic footprint, product portfolio and adjacent operating workflows            |
| Access               | Is there a credible route into the account?                                            | CRM history, approved usage data, existing contacts, mutual connections, partners, investors, customers, events and executive relationships |

#### Tier The Accounts

| Tier | Prospecting approach |
|---|---|
| Tier 1 | One-to-one account strategy, deep research, custom workflow hypothesis, stakeholder map and coordinated multichannel outreach |
| Tier 2 | One-to-few campaign for accounts sharing the same industry, company type, workflow and signals |
| Tier 3 | Scaled coverage or monitoring until stronger evidence appears |

A recognizable logo would not automatically become Tier 1. A smaller account with a visible workflow, timely signals, identifiable owner and credible technical capacity may be more likely to progress.

#### Turn Research Into An Account Hypothesis

For every priority account, I would produce the same chain:

| Step | Question |
|---|---|
| Signal | What changed? |
| Interpretation | Why might that change matter? |
| Workflow hypothesis | What work could OpenAI improve? |
| Product hypothesis | Which OpenAI surface might fit? |
| Stakeholder | Who probably owns, implements or benefits from it? |
| Message | Why is this relevant to that person now? |
| Next step | What small discovery or proof step should we earn? |

For example:

> If a digital-native fintech is expanding its engineering organization, hires a developer-productivity leader and already experiments with coding agents, I would investigate a Codex hypothesis with engineering leadership.
>
> If a traditional bank announces an AI-transformation programme and operates a high-volume support organization, I might investigate a governed customer-operations or voice workflow with business, technology and risk stakeholders.

#### Run Coordinated, Problem-First Outreach

> I would treat the account as the unit of orchestration and the stakeholder as the unit of outreach. Each priority stakeholder would receive a complete, persona-specific cadence, while every interaction would remain coordinated around one account hypothesis.
>
> My first message would contain one relevant signal, one workflow hypothesis, one reason it matters to that stakeholder and one small ask. I would use calls, email, LinkedIn, events, partners and warm introductions where appropriate.

For a Tier 1 enterprise account, I would normally activate three VP-level threads on the same day:

| Stakeholder archetype | Question being tested |
|---|---|
| Business-outcome VP | Is the problem strategically important? |
| Operational VP | Is the workflow frequent, expensive or measurable? |
| Technical VP | Is the workflow technically feasible and sufficiently resourced? |

The titles would change with the workflow:

- For customer service: VP Customer Experience, VP Service Operations and VP AI or Digital.
- For Codex: VP Engineering, VP Developer Productivity and the relevant VP of Infrastructure or Security.
- For knowledge work: the VP of the business function, VP Transformation and VP IT or AI.

I would not wait for one VP to reject the hypothesis before approaching another. The three threads would begin together as one coordinated account motion, with the same underlying workflow translated into the outcome each stakeholder owns.

##### Use A Consistent Multichannel Cadence

For a Tier 1 account, I would begin with a ten-touch cadence over approximately 22 business days. Ten sits inside Gong's recommended range of six to twelve touches, but it would remain a starting hypothesis rather than a universal rule. Salesloft's analysis of 3.4 million cadences found that 80% of its top 100 cadences began with a call followed by an email.

The purpose of the cadence is not to repeat the same ask ten times. It is to develop the account thesis and make the value increasingly tangible:

> Establish relevance → document the hypothesis → add a non-obvious insight → test the direct route → quantify the consequence → invite correction → establish proof proximity → map the workflow → build a personalized concept demo → resolve the account

| Touch | Day | Channel                                  | Message purpose                                                                                                                                                                                  | New value contributed                                                                                          | Small ask                                                                                  |
| ----: | --: | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |

|     2 |   1 | Personalized email                       | **Document the hypothesis.** Give the stakeholder a short written version they can inspect or forward.                                                                                           | One observation, its likely workflow implication, one credible proof point and one question                    | “Have I understood the problem correctly?”                                                 |
|     3 |   3 | LinkedIn                                 | **Add a non-obvious insight.** Show that the outreach is based on more than repeating a public announcement.                                                                                     | A relevant operating pattern, technical implication or adjacent market observation                             | “Is this how you are thinking about the initiative?”                                       |
|     4 |   5 | WhatsApp, where appropriate              | **Test the direct route and make response easy.** Use a short, human message to check ownership or preferred channel rather than paste the product pitch again.                                  | A low-friction summary of why the question matters to that person                                              | “Is this yours, or is there someone better to ask?”                                        |
|     5 |   8 | Email                                    | **Make the consequence concrete.** Translate the workflow problem into the cost, delay, risk, capacity constraint, revenue impact or customer outcome that this VP owns.                         | A preliminary implication chain or simple sensitivity model with assumptions labeled                           | “Which consequence would matter most if the hypothesis is real?”                           |

The workflow map and personalized concept demo are deliberately late in the cold cadence. The internal research warns that generic collateral and broad demos are easy to ignore. The artifact should feel like the buyer's work, make its assumptions visible and invite correction. It should use public or synthetic information until the customer authorizes access, and it should never imply that OpenAI has already validated the production architecture.

WhatsApp would replace the voicemail step only when the number had been obtained legitimately and direct business messaging fit the relationship and local norm. Otherwise I would substitute LinkedIn or another approved channel.

The channels and timing would be adjusted using OpenAI's internal conversion data, the buyer's preferences and legitimate regional norms. Every touch should add new information, evidence, an artifact or a different stakeholder perspective; I would not fill the cadence with repeated "bumping this" messages.

##### Translate The Account Thesis By Persona

The same workflow should sound different to each stakeholder:

| Persona | Message |
|---|---|
| Business-outcome VP | Why the workflow matters strategically and what customer or business outcome could improve |
| Operational VP | Where the workflow creates measurable cost, delay, manual work or inconsistency |
| Technical VP | Whether the organization can implement it and how OpenAI would complement existing systems |
| Security or Risk VP | What data, access, actions, permissions and controls would be required |
| Finance VP | Whether the expected outcome is sufficiently material to justify the investment |

The objective is to multi-thread around a coherent account thesis—not send unrelated product messages to every department. Demandbase recommends engaging multiple relevant contacts simultaneously, while 6sense describes the model as one account motion serving different messages to different personas.

##### Respond At The Account Level

The objective is not to complete every scheduled touch. It is to create a qualified account conversation.

| Signal | Action |
|---|---|
| Positive response | Pause that stakeholder's cadence and begin a direct conversation |
| Referral | Close the incorrect thread and approach the referred owner with context |
| Useful correction | Update the account hypothesis and remaining stakeholder messages |
| Role-specific rejection | Continue the other relevant threads unless the objection applies account-wide |
| Account-wide objection | Pause the campaign and reassess qualification or timing |
| No response | Complete the cadence, close the loop respectfully and return the account to monitoring |
| Champion emerges | Replace cold outreach with coordinated introductions and working sessions |

For Tier 1 accounts, I would coordinate the stakeholders, messages and response plan with the Account Director. I would usually enter with one primary workflow and perhaps one secondary hypothesis. I would only run unrelated departmental plays simultaneously when the account was sufficiently large, each hypothesis was independently credible and the approach was aligned with the Account Director.

#### Qualify Curiosity Versus Readiness

Discovery would test what public research cannot:

- What workflow are we changing?
- Who owns it?
- Where does it break today?
- What measurable outcome matters?
- What systems contain the necessary context?
- Who would implement the solution?
- What data, security and approval requirements apply?
- What proof would justify the next investment?
- What is the next concrete technical or commercial milestone?

> A company can have an attractive use case but still lack the capacity to execute it. I would not forecast the opportunity confidently until there was a real owner, implementation path and agreed next step.

#### Hand The Account Director A Real Opportunity

My handoff would explain:

- Why this account?
- Why now?
- What is the workflow?
- Who matters?
- What evidence supports the hypothesis?
- What did discovery confirm?
- What proof is still missing?
- What should happen next?

> The goal is to make the Account Director smarter before entering the account, not simply busier.

#### Turn Campaigns Into A Learning System

> I would track which observable signals produce qualified opportunities, which industry and workflow combinations progress, and which apparent signals create false positives.
>
> Every response, meeting, win and loss should improve the segmentation, scoring, product routing, messages, discovery questions and forecasting criteria.
>
> AI would help with research, signal monitoring, meeting preparation and CRM hygiene, but the objective would be to increase judgment density—not merely message volume.

#### Proof From Airwallex

> This is similar to what I did at Airwallex. I did not stop at deciding to target travel companies. I divided travel into different operating models and discovered that outbound tour operators had the clearest overseas-payment problem.
>
> That gave me a sharper ICP, visible account signals, the right stakeholders, a narrow payout wedge and an expansion path into cards and payment acceptance.
>
> At OpenAI, I would apply the same discipline. I would organize the market by industry, distinguish traditional enterprises from digital natives, identify the highest-value workflow and prioritize accounts showing observable evidence that the timing may be right.
>
> The research earns the conversation. Discovery determines whether the customer is ready. The strongest opportunities are where meaningful value, timely signals, credible ownership and execution capacity come together.

### 3. Is top-down or bottom-up prospecting better?

> For OpenAI, I would lead top-down, normally starting with the VP who owns the business problem.
>
> For Codex, that might be the VP of Engineering. For a customer-support agent, it could be the VP of Customer Experience. For broad employee adoption, it might be the VP of IT or Transformation.
>
> I would start at VP altitude with the functional leader who owns the outcome while simultaneously multithreading horizontally into the operational and technical VPs required to validate and execute the workflow.

| Stakeholder          | Why they matter                                                                |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Functional VP        | Owns the problem and target outcome                                            |
| Technology VP        | Owns the systems, data and implementation resources                            |
| Security VP          | Determines whether the proposed access, actions and controls are secure enough |
| Finance VP           | Confirms that the value justifies the investment                               |
| Senior functional VP | Resolves priorities and creates permission to scale                            |
| Practitioners        | Confirm that the workflow will work in reality                                 |
|                      |                                                                                |

The Security VP is particularly important because OpenAI systems may access company data, source code and internal tools, and agents may also take actions.

For Codex, the Security VP will care about repository access, code execution, permissions and how sensitive code is handled. For a customer-service agent, they will care about customer data, identity, system access, audit trails and which actions require human approval.

> I would treat the Security VP as an approval and design partner, not a late-stage blocker. Involving security early helps us design a workflow the company can actually deploy.
>
> Once the leadership coalition is aligned, I would work with technical teams and practitioners to validate the integrations, operating workflow and adoption requirements. The Account Director and I could then consolidate the evidence and bring the final case to the appropriate executive sponsor.
>
> The motion is: start with the VP who owns the outcome, align the executives who make it executable, validate it with the people doing the work and consolidate the business case upward.

Evidence from my experience:

> At UNA, I began with the Head of Partnerships because that person owned the platform-expansion initiative. I then widened the conversation horizontally into Product, Engineering, Legal, Compliance and Risk before consolidating the broader case with the CEO.

Short version:

> Top-down is the better entry point for OpenAI because senior ownership creates priority and resources. But the deal becomes real only when technology, security, finance and practitioners are aligned around the same workflow.

### 3A. What objections would you expect from a CISO, and how would you address them?

> The CISO owns the company's cybersecurity risk. The CIO may ask whether the organization can implement the technology; the CISO asks whether it can do so without exposing the company, its customers or its data to unacceptable risk.
>
> This is commercially critical because the CISO can effectively stop a deployment. A business executive may support the use case and agree with the ROI, but it cannot move into production unless security is comfortable with the architecture and controls. Involving the CISO early helps turn an exciting AI use case into something the company can actually deploy.
>
> I would first ask what the CISO is protecting, what data and systems the workflow needs, what actions the AI could take, and which internal policy or regulatory requirement concerns them. I would then bring in the appropriate OpenAI security or technical resource and address the objection against the customer's exact product surface, configuration, contract and region.

The four major concerns are:

- **Data exposure:** Could confidential customer, employee, financial or source-code data leak?
- **Unauthorized action:** Could an agent access the wrong system or take a damaging action?
- **Compliance:** Does the deployment meet the company's security, privacy, regulatory, retention and residency obligations?
- **Accountability:** If something goes wrong, can the company reconstruct what happened, determine who was responsible and respond?

| Likely CISO objection | What the concern means | How I would respond with OpenAI |
|---|---|---|
| “Will our data be used to train models?” | Confidential information could become exposed or used outside the intended purpose | For ChatGPT Enterprise, OpenAI documents no training on business data by default and encryption in transit and at rest. I would confirm the exact commitment for the customer's product surface and agreement rather than generalize across every service. |
| “Who can access the system?” | Unauthorized employees or former employees could access sensitive tools and data | Depending on plan and configuration, enterprise identity controls can include SSO, domain verification, SCIM provisioning, lifecycle management, group synchronization and RBAC. I would map access to the customer's identity groups and least-privilege roles. |
| “What can the agent do?” | An agent could take an unauthorized or irreversible action | Start with a bounded workflow and the least privilege required. OpenAI documents connector action controls, read-only or custom action sets, approval behavior and per-agent controls. Sensitive writes should remain approval-gated until the customer has sufficient evidence and trust. |
| “What happens when external tools are connected?” | A connector or third party creates another route for data leakage or unauthorized action | Review both OpenAI's controls and the connected service's scopes, permissions, retention, logging and residency. Users remain limited by source-system permissions, while administrators can govern plugin availability, connector actions and approval behavior. |
| “Can we investigate what happened?” | Security needs records for audit, legal, governance and incident response | OpenAI documents supported audit logging and a Compliance API for auditable security, legal, governance and investigation workflows. I would validate the exact event coverage required instead of promising that every prompt, file or tool action is universally included. |
| “Can Codex read or execute too much?” | A coding agent may reach sensitive files, repositories, networks, commands or credentials | Codex supports admin-enforced managed configuration, permission profiles, sandbox restrictions, filesystem read denials, network allow and deny rules, command rules, approval policies and MCP allowlists. I would begin with a narrow repository and controlled permissions rather than broad production access. |
| “Where is our data stored, and for how long?” | The customer may have retention, residency or regulatory requirements | OpenAI provides retention and residency controls for supported plans and surfaces, but coverage is not universal across every feature, endpoint, region or agreement. I would verify the customer's exact residency, retention and compliance requirements with the relevant OpenAI specialist. |
| “What if the model or agent makes a mistake?” | Incorrect output could create operational, financial or security consequences | Constrain the workflow, separate read access from action permissions, use structured outputs where appropriate, test realistic cases, preserve logs, and require human review for high-impact or low-confidence actions. Start with a reversible proof before expanding autonomy. |

The sales posture:

> I would not answer a security objection with “trust us” or treat it as a documentation exercise. I would turn it into a design question: what is the minimum data, access and autonomy required to prove this workflow safely?
>

### OpenAI-Specific Commercial Judgment

10. How would you explain OpenAI's value without giving the customer a product catalogue?
11. What are companies currently misunderstanding about enterprise AI adoption?

> The biggest misunderstanding is treating enterprise AI adoption like a conventional software rollout.
>
> A company buys seats, gives employees access, runs training and launches a few pilots. That can generate activity, but activity is not the same as adoption—and adoption is not automatically business value.
>
> Real adoption happens when AI becomes part of a repeatable workflow that produces a trusted, measurable outcome.
>
> That requires three things.
>
> **First, workflow ownership.** There needs to be a specific process, a baseline, a business outcome and someone accountable for improving it. “Increase AI usage” is not enough. “Reduce reconciliation investigations from four hours to thirty minutes” is much more useful.
>
> **Second, clear operating rules.** Companies need to decide what data the AI can access, who can use it, which actions it can take independently, which actions require human approval and how accurate the output must be before it can be used. They also need a record of what the AI saw and did, a process for investigating mistakes and a person who remains accountable for the outcome. These rules should be designed into the workflow from the beginning rather than added after a pilot.
>
> **Third, operating change.** The workflow has to fit how people actually work. Users need to understand when to trust the system, when to review it and how to provide feedback. Those interactions should then improve the prompts, tools, context and evals over time.
>
> I learned this while building Sunder for a law firm. Senior leadership being interested in AI did not create adoption. The intake specialists, paralegals and lawyers had to trust the output inside their actual workflow. We narrowed the promise to evidence preparation, showed citations and validation flags, preserved human review and introduced it gradually with the people closest to the work.
>
> From a sales perspective, I would therefore qualify more than pain and budget. I would ask: Who owns the workflow? What measurable outcome are we changing? Who will implement it? Who must trust the result? What is the AI allowed to do? And what happens after the pilot succeeds?
>
> My summary would be: **access creates experimentation; workflow redesign creates enterprise value.**

## Part IV — Canonical TruTrip Competitive-Win Story

### Public Account Context

[TruTrip](https://trutrip.co/en-sg/) currently presents itself as a corporate-travel management platform for Singapore businesses. Its public offering includes travel inventory, automated policies and approvals, reporting, spend control, payments and finance integrations, and high-touch customer support. That makes payments an operational input into a broader travel workflow rather than an isolated checkout feature.

This public context explains why TruTrip was relevant, but the deal narrative below is grounded in Seth's user-confirmed lived account history rather than the current website.

### Deal Context

Navan was already an Airwallex customer. I watched the relevant Chorus calls and reviewed the deal insights recorded in our CRM to understand how a technically sophisticated travel platform could use Airwallex across customer payments and supplier fulfilment.

I wanted to challenge myself by pursuing a more technical platform opportunity, so I brought the TruTrip account to my manager. My manager personally knew TruTrip's Head of Finance and made the introduction.

That combination gave me:

- A relevant travel-platform proof point.
- A hypothesis about TruTrip's payment and supplier-fulfilment architecture.
- A warm introduction to the person who ultimately became my Finance champion.

The introduction created access; it did not create the opportunity. I still needed to diagnose a valuable problem, build the buying group, overcome the incumbent and make the migration defensible.

### 87. Give me the overview of the TruTrip win.

> TruTrip is one of my strongest examples of consultative, value-based land and expand. Stripe was already integrated and working, so there was significant technical inertia. We ultimately displaced Stripe completely by landing through like-for-like settlement and better reconciliation, then built a scale-triggered expansion roadmap around programmatic virtual cards and supplier payouts.

### 88. What did discovery uncover?

I traced the complete financial lifecycle of a travel booking:

- In which currency did the customer pay?
- In which currency did TruTrip receive settlement?
- How did TruTrip pay the corresponding airline or hotel?
- How many providers and people touched the transaction?
- How did Finance reconcile the customer payment with the supplier expense?

That uncovered two connected problems:

1. Unnecessary payment, FX and transfer costs.
2. Fragmented operational and reconciliation data.

#### Double conversion

TruTrip's home currency was SGD. An Australian customer might pay in AUD while the corresponding Australian supplier also needed to receive AUD.

```text
Customer pays AUD
→ Stripe converts AUD into SGD
→ TruTrip receives SGD
→ TruTrip's bank converts SGD back into AUD
→ Supplier receives AUD over SWIFT
```

The same money completed an unnecessary round trip:

```text
AUD → SGD → AUD
```

TruTrip incurred the initial payment-processing cost, an AUD-to-SGD conversion, its bank's SGD-to-AUD spread, SWIFT or intermediary fees and less control over each booking's margin.

With Airwallex like-for-like settlement:

```text
Customer pays AUD
→ Airwallex settles the proceeds into TruTrip's AUD balance
→ TruTrip retains AUD
→ AUD remains available for Australian obligations
```

### 89. What was the land?

The Head of Finance became my champion. We landed with the payment-gateway migration because it created the largest immediate and measurable Finance return.

The initial scope was:

- Replace Stripe payment acceptance.
- Implement like-for-like settlement.
- Hold relevant currencies in Airwallex.
- Use Airwallex for manual supplier transfers.
- Consolidate more of the payment and reconciliation data.

The land stood on its own economically. It did not depend on TruTrip implementing programmatic supplier payments immediately.

### 90. How did you quantify the ROI?

I worked with the Finance lead to build a pure ROI case using TruTrip's payment volume, currency mix and existing workflow.

| ROI component | Value |
|---|---|
| Lower historical processing cost | Immediate saving on relevant payment volume |
| Like-for-like settlement | Avoided automatic conversion into SGD |
| Avoided second conversion | Retained AUD could fund AUD obligations |
| Reduced SWIFT usage | Lower transfer and intermediary costs where local rails applied |
| Workflow consolidation | Less time moving between platforms and preparing data |
| Reconciliation improvement | Less manual joining and exception investigation |

Under the historical deal-era pricing, the initial acceptance and settlement improvement was approximately 1.1 percentage points on eligible foreign-currency volume before including the avoided second conversion and SWIFT costs.

```text
Payment and FX savings
+ avoided transfer costs
+ workflow and reconciliation savings
− migration cost
= first-year net value
```

I kept harder-to-prove upside, such as increased booking capacity, outside the core ROI calculation. That made the CEO case conservative and defensible.

### 91. What did consolidation mean?

Before Airwallex, one booking could create records across Stripe, bank statements, FX records, SWIFT transfers, company-card statements, TruTrip's booking platform and its accounting system.

Finance had to export and join those records to determine what the customer paid, what fees were deducted, what the supplier received and the actual margin on the booking.

TruTrip could use the same booking ID across the incoming customer payment and outgoing supplier transfer:

| Financial event | TruTrip identifier |
|---|---|
| Incoming customer payment | Booking ID used as the order ID |
| Outgoing supplier transfer | Same booking ID used as the transfer reference |

| Transaction | Order ID | Reference | Amount |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Customer payment | `TT-48219` | — | +AUD 1,000 |
| Payment fees | `TT-48219` | — | −AUD 43 |
| Supplier transfer | — | `TT-48219` | −AUD 700 |
| Transfer fee | — | `TT-48219` | −AUD 2 |

Finance could filter Airwallex's transaction-reconciliation data for `TT-48219` and trace the customer revenue, fees and supplier payment without joining separate Stripe, bank and SWIFT reports.

Initially, employees entered the booking reference manually. The immediate value was better consolidation and traceability, not complete automation.

### 92. What was the immediate operational value?

The initial operational value was fewer platforms and less reconciliation work. TruTrip still executed supplier payments manually.

#### Before Airwallex

```text
Check customer payment in Stripe
→ check settlement in the bank
→ review the FX conversion
→ access the bank or card platform
→ execute the supplier payment manually
→ retrieve the SWIFT or card confirmation
→ update TruTrip's booking platform
→ export reports from several systems
→ reconcile them in a spreadsheet or accounting system
```

#### Initial Airwallex workflow

```text
Check customer payment in Airwallex
→ see settlement and retained currency
→ use the relevant wallet balance
→ initiate the supplier transfer manually
→ enter the TruTrip booking reference
→ follow the approval process
→ see the outgoing payment in Airwallex
→ filter reconciliation data by booking reference
```

Operations and Finance still selected the booking, confirmed the supplier, initiated and approved the payment, entered the reference and resolved exceptions. The improvement was that more of the workflow happened within one platform.

| Immediate improvement | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Fewer platforms | Less movement between Stripe, bank portals and payment systems |
| Fewer exports | Less data preparation before reconciliation |
| Common booking reference | Easier matching of revenue and supplier expenditure |
| Consistent approvals | Familiar creator and approver controls |
| Better status visibility | Easier to see whether payments were pending or completed |
| Simpler investigations | Fees, currencies and transactions were easier to trace together |

> Airwallex did not initially remove TruTrip's manual supplier-payment process. It reduced the operational overhead around that process by consolidating payments, currency balances, transfers, approvals and reconciliation data into one platform.

### 93. Who was in the buying group?

| Stakeholder | Position | How I addressed it |
|---|---|---|
| Head of Finance | Champion | Co-authored the consolidation, reconciliation and ROI case |
| Technical lead | Principal blocker | Narrowed phase one, brought internal technical support and connected the work to TruTrip's product ambitions |
| Operations | Supportive but cautious | Preserved the initial manual workflow and reflected familiar approval controls inside Airwallex |
| CEO | Economic buyer and final decision-maker | Presented immediate ROI, controlled transition risk and long-term strategic relevance |

### 94. How did you overcome Engineering's objection?

Stripe already worked, and TruTrip's technical lead saw replacing it as a distraction from the existing product roadmap.

I broke the implementation into phases:

| Phase | Scope | Business reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Gateway migration | Replace Stripe and implement like-for-like settlement | Capture the largest immediate Finance benefit |
| 2. Stabilize the foundation | Validate payments, refunds, settlement and reporting | Ensure the new workflow was reliable |
| 3. Measure operational scale | Quantify supplier-payment volume and manual work | Establish whether automation justified Engineering time |
| 4. Programmatic payments | Add virtual cards and supplier payouts | Automate fulfilment when sufficient scale was reached |

I also brought in Airwallex's internal technical team to help scope the integration, map booking identifiers and payment objects, test payment and refund scenarios, validate settlement behaviour and resolve implementation issues.

My role was to coordinate the technical engagement and connect implementation decisions to the business case, not to write TruTrip's integration myself.

### 95. How did you address Operations' transition risk?

Operations supported the longer-term automation opportunity but worried about disrupting live bookings and changing familiar payment controls.

We kept the initial change narrow:

```text
Phase one:
Migrate customer payments and settlement
→ preserve manual supplier payments

Later:
Add programmatic cards and payouts
→ automate once scale justifies it
```

We also showed how TruTrip's existing bank approval structure could be reflected inside Airwallex through defined payment creators, separate approvers, permission controls, payment thresholds and familiar review sequences.

The platform changed, but the governance model remained familiar. TruTrip did not have to redesign its controls at the same time it changed providers.

### 96. What was the expansion roadmap?

We discussed TruTrip's ambition to compete with platforms such as Navan. At sufficient booking volume, manually initiating every airline and hotel payment would become a structural constraint.

I coordinated a working session between Airwallex's product lead and TruTrip's technical lead to demonstrate how Airwallex could support the future architecture.

#### Supplier accepts cards

```text
Booking confirmed
→ TruTrip creates a controlled virtual card
→ card receives the required amount and controls
→ card pays the airline or hotel
→ transaction connects back to the booking
```

#### Supplier requires bank payment

```text
Booking confirmed
→ TruTrip identifies the supplier beneficiary
→ TruTrip initiates a payout
→ Airwallex sends funds through the appropriate rail
→ transaction connects back to the booking
```

Programmatic payments could automate payment creation, booking-reference entry, controls, status updates and booking-to-expense linkage. We did not force that scope into the initial implementation. TruTrip would make the additional engineering investment only when booking volume and manual workload created sufficient return.

### 97. What was the outcome?

TruTrip completely migrated its payment gateway from Stripe to Airwallex.

We won the land through:

- Like-for-like settlement.
- Reduced double conversion and transfer costs.
- Better booking-level reconciliation.
- A quantified ROI case.
- A tightly scoped migration.
- Familiar operational controls.

We earned the expansion roadmap by showing how the same infrastructure could support programmatic cards and supplier payouts as TruTrip scaled.

### 98. What did the TruTrip win teach you?

> Replacing an embedded incumbent required more than proving that Airwallex was cheaper. I had to understand the complete workflow, quantify the immediate value, build a champion in Finance, work through Engineering's opportunity-cost objection, protect Operations during the transition and connect the initial implementation to the CEO's longer-term ambitions.
>
> My land-and-expand principle is to land on a painful problem that creates measurable value on its own, then expand only when proven usage, trust and customer scale justify the next investment.
