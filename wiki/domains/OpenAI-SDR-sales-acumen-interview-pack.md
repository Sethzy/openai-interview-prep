# OpenAI SDR Sales Acumen Interview Pack

## Purpose

This is the execution pack for Phase 3A: the recruiter's 30-minute Sales Acumen interview with an Account Director.

It has one job: make Seth legible as a serious seller who can identify the right account, open it, run discovery, qualify it, multi-thread it, navigate risk, create commercial momentum, and give an Account Director a high-conviction opportunity.

Technical preparation belongs in [[OpenAI-SDR-technical-acumen-interview-pack]]. Do not add product encyclopaedia material here.

### Source Precedence

1. The recruiter-screen learnings define what this round is testing and are the canonical process source.
2. The hiring-leader debrief defines the answer-control failures, evidence gaps and repair rules.
3. Seth's direct corrections dated 2026-07-20 govern the commercial numbers in this pack and replace older CV or answer-bank figures.
4. The comprehensive answer bank supplies candidate language and examples, but does not override a later verified correction.
5. Seth Second Brain GTM pages supply sales-process doctrine only. They do not establish facts about Seth's employment, accounts, quota or results.

## Delivery Rules

1. **Answer the noun first.** Persona, company, number, role, result, then context.
2. **Initial answer: 30–60 seconds.** Deal walkthrough: 90 seconds maximum.
3. **One metric per sentence.** State the basis when needed.
4. **Never improvise a number.** Use the placeholder language below.
5. **Sales first, technical depth second.** Technical fluency supports the sale; it is not the story.
6. **Stop when the question is answered.** Let the interviewer pull more detail.
7. **Use “I” for ownership and “we” for team execution.**

> “Let me answer that directly first.”

> [!warning] Precision line
> “I want to separate the number I can stand behind from the part I still need to verify.”

## The Shared Prospecting-To-Close Framework

Use the same progression for both flagship stories. It makes the comparison easy for the interviewer and proves that the result came from a repeatable operating system.

| Gate | Question the answer must resolve | Evidence that earns progression |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Role and scoreboard | What were you responsible for, how were you measured and what did you deliver? | Precise scope, metric basis and result |
| 2. Territory and ICP | Which accounts deserved seller time, and which did not? | Finite account universe, ICP and anti-ICP |
| 3. Account selection | Why this account above the others? | Fit, value, timing and messageability |
| 4. Research and point of view | What did you learn that the buyer would find useful? | Offer-specific insight, not a generic trigger |
| 5. Stakeholder entry and outbound | Who did you target first, why them and how did you earn attention? | Persona ownership and a relevant opening |
| 6. Discovery | What business problem, consequence and priority did you validate? | Customer-specific current state and cost of inaction |
| 7. Qualification | Why was this a real opportunity rather than interest? | Problem, priority, people, process and proof |
| 8. Champion and multi-threading | Who sold internally, who could say no and how did you widen access? | Champion behavior and a role-specific stakeholder map |
| 9. Proof and objections | What did the buyer need to believe, and how did you reduce risk? | Buyer-defined proof and resolved objections |
| 10. Business case | How did each stakeholder justify action? | Executive outcome, owner metric, user workflow and credible risk plan |
| 11. Commercial process and close | How did you control scope, negotiation, procurement and signature? | Mutual next steps, owned blockers and a defensible agreement |
| 12. Onboarding and expansion | How did value move from signature to use and broader adoption? | Controlled activation, realized value and earned expansion |
| 13. Ownership and learning | What did you personally do, and what became repeatable? | Clear “I” ownership, result and lesson |

> [!tip] The operating principle
> A deal advances on buyer evidence, not seller optimism. A meeting, demo or proposal is activity; a named priority, buyer-edited case, stakeholder access, agreed proof criterion or owned commercial next step is progress.

## Flagship Case One: Boxo And UNA Financial

### 1. Role And Scoreboard

**What were you responsible for, how were you measured and what did you deliver?**

> I joined Boxo as the first business hire and grew from outbound into founding enterprise sales leadership. Because it was an early founder-led motion, there was no mature individual quota plan. I owned account selection, prospecting, discovery, stakeholder mapping, product and engineering coordination, pricing, commercial negotiation and close. The clean commercial result is approximately USD 600K ARR across UNA Financial and Epon after UNA expanded, plus nine strategic super-app partnerships. I use UNA as the detailed case because I can explain the complete enterprise motion.

**Numbers to land:** UNA started at approximately USD 200K ARR and expanded to approximately USD 300K ARR. Epon was another approximately USD 300K ARR account. Nine strategic super-app partnerships.

### 2. Territory And ICP

**How did you define the territory, ICP and anti-ICP?**

> I treated APAC as a finite strategic-account universe, not a list of every company with an app. My first screen was approximately ten million downloads across the major app stores. Within that, I prioritized banks, e-wallets, exchanges, marketplaces and consumer platforms with a wallet or payment layer, multi-vertical ambition and a reason to add third-party services without building every integration internally. I deprioritized smaller or single-purpose apps, accounts without ecosystem ambition and companies with no partner-supply problem or credible path to action.

| Tier | Account conditions | Seller motion |
|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 | Approximately 10M+ downloads; wallet/payment layer; multi-vertical ambition; public expansion signal; named owner; credible timing | Manual research, persona-specific outreach and coordinated multi-threading |
| Tier 2 | Structural fit but weak timing, access or named vertical priority | Targeted nurture and signal monitoring |
| Deprioritized | Below scale, single-purpose, no ecosystem ambition, no partner-supply problem or no path to action | Preserve seller time until something changes |

### 3. Account Selection

**Why was UNA a Tier 1 account?**

> Scale made UNA eligible; timing made it actionable. It fit the fintech and embedded-services profile, and it had publicly signaled expansion into eSIMs. That gave me a specific vertical, an active business priority and a likely commercial owner. Compared with a structurally similar account that had no current initiative, UNA justified deeper manual research because I could see both potential value and a credible reason to act.

### 4. Research And Point Of View

**What did you learn that the buyer would find useful?**

> I treated the eSIM announcement as a routing signal, not the outreach message. Before contacting UNA, I identified relevant eSIM providers and spoke with the supply side. That let me approach the buyer with partner context and a credible launch path. The point of view was that Boxo could help UNA launch this category and create reusable infrastructure for future embedded services, rather than forcing a bespoke integration every time.

### 5. Stakeholder Entry And Outbound

**Who did you target first, why them and how did you earn attention?**

> I entered through the commercial or partnerships stakeholder responsible for securing the eSIM supply. That was the right first persona because they owned the immediate business problem. My opening was based on relevant partner context, not a generic product pitch. Once there was alignment, I deliberately expanded into product and engineering for feasibility, then executive and risk stakeholders for strategic value and vendor confidence.

> [!warning] Truth boundary
> Do not invent the exact outbound channel or touch count. The verified strength of the story is the supply-side research, correct persona and useful opening.

### 6. Discovery

**What business problem, consequence and priority did you validate?**

> The immediate problem was how to launch eSIMs and other third-party services without taking on a bespoke native integration for every category. Commercial needed partner supply and speed. Product needed a coherent in-app experience. Engineering needed a feasible architecture and clear support ownership. At the executive level, the initiative connected to engagement, monetization and broader super-app expansion. That turned a feature conversation into a business-priority conversation.

**What would have disqualified the opportunity?**

> I would have slowed it down if the expansion were only exploratory, if there were no accountable owner, if the product and engineering teams rejected the integration model or if the account wanted custom development without a credible commercial commitment.

### 7. Qualification

**Why was this a real opportunity rather than interest?**

> I qualify around problem, priority, people, process and proof. UNA had a real launch priority and a commercial owner. We could identify the product, engineering, executive and risk stakeholders required for a decision. The main proof questions were integration feasibility and whether a young vendor could support a reusable embedded-services layer. The opportunity earned progression because each meeting resolved a decision risk and widened the buying group, rather than merely generating enthusiasm.

### 8. Champion And Multi-Threading

**Who was the champion, who could say no and how did you widen access?**

> The commercial or partnerships stakeholder was my champion because the eSIM launch was their priority and I was helping them secure a workable path. I used that shared objective to multi-thread rather than going around them. The Product Head in the Philippines cared about experience and roadmap. Engineering in Ukraine cared about feasibility, authentication, payments and ownership. The CEO in Singapore cared about expansion, engagement and monetization. Legal, compliance and risk could stop the deal if data flow, SLAs, liability or vendor maturity remained unclear.

**How do you know this was a champion rather than a friendly contact?**

> They had an accountable business outcome, helped progress the internal process and gave us a path into the stakeholders needed to evaluate the decision. The test was behavior and access, not enthusiasm.

### 9. Proof And Objections

**What did the buyer need to believe, and what was the hardest objection?**

> The hardest objection was build versus buy combined with vendor maturity. Engineering could reasonably ask why it should trust a young vendor's SDK or why it should not build internally. I did not claim they lacked the capability. I reframed the comparison around launch speed, reuse across multiple categories, maintenance burden and displaced roadmap work. Then I translated every external concern into a precise product or implementation question internally and returned with clear boundaries on integration, support, data flow and ownership.

**Why did you win?**

> We made the decision defensible: a useful commercial insight, a feasible product path, clear technical and risk answers and an expansion model that justified the integration effort.

### 10. Business Case

**How did you connect the technology to business value?**

> The executive case was faster multi-vertical expansion, greater engagement and new monetization. The commercial case was a repeatable way to add partner supply. The product case was a coherent embedded experience. The engineering case was avoiding a growing portfolio of bespoke integrations and maintenance. The risk case was explicit responsibility, support and contractual boundaries. The business case worked because each stakeholder could defend the same decision in their own language.

### 11. Commercial Process And Close

**How did you manage negotiation, legal and the close?**

> I owned pricing and commercial negotiation and coordinated the legal and risk process. The core discipline was to keep commercial scope, implementation ownership, support, SLAs, data flow and liability tied to named owners. I did not treat a proposal as progress by itself; progress meant a blocker had an owner, the next decision was explicit and the buying group had enough confidence to proceed.

> [!warning] Truth boundary
> The exact sales-cycle length and specific concession history are not verified. Do not manufacture a month count, discount or give-get.

### 12. Onboarding And Expansion

**How did the account expand after the initial agreement?**

> I positioned eSIMs as the first use case inside a broader embedded-services strategy. Once the buyer had confidence in the model, the relationship expanded across additional categories such as ecommerce, travel and other services. UNA began at approximately USD 200K ARR and expanded to approximately USD 300K ARR. That expansion is important because it shows the initial sale created a reusable platform relationship rather than a one-off integration.

### 13. Ownership And Learning

**What did you personally own, and what became repeatable?**

> I selected and qualified the account, developed the point of view, opened the commercial relationship, mapped and multi-threaded the buying group, translated technical and risk questions, built the value case, handled pricing and commercial negotiation and drove expansion. The repeatable lesson was that a public signal identifies where to investigate; useful insight earns the first conversation; and enterprise progression comes from removing a different decision risk for each stakeholder.

> I reviewed the tour groups and destinations EU Holidays actively marketed, mapped those destinations to the payment rails it would need and identified where Airwallex had stronger corridor coverage while competitors were weaker, especially in harder markets and during peak periods. **Who did you target first, why them and how did you earn attention?**

> Finance was the correct entry point because it owned the payment workflow, cost, reconciliation and control. I reached the Head of Finance and could discuss the company's actual destination corridors and operating risk. That relevance earned the conversation; the physical channel simply created access.

### 6. Discovery

**What business problem, consequence and priority did you validate?**

> I validated more than FX price. Finance cared about transfer cost, reconciliation and control. Operations cared about urgent overseas payments succeeding during live tours. Commercial cared about serving destination markets without corridor limitations. The consequence of failure was operational disruption during seasonal peaks, not merely a few basis points of cost. That made reliability and provider responsiveness part of the business case.

**What would have disqualified the opportunity?**

> I would have slowed it down if there were no meaningful cross-border volume, no recurring corridor pain, no finance owner willing to test a new provider or no safe initial workload through which we could prove value.

### 7. Qualification

**Why was this a real opportunity rather than interest?**

> The problem was recurring and tied to a core operating workflow. The Head of Finance owned it and could help us map the wider decision. We identified operations and commercial as affected stakeholders and the CFO and CEO as final decision-makers. We also had a low-risk proof path: start with FX and controlled volume, establish reliability and earn expansion. That gave us problem, priority, people, process and proof rather than an interested meeting with no route to action.

### 8. Champion And Multi-Threading

**Who was the champion, who could say no and how did you widen access?**

> The Head of Finance was my champion because the account's payment performance, cost and controls sat inside their remit. I worked through that relationship to involve the Head of Operations and Head of Commercial. Operations could validate the consequence of payment failure; commercial could validate the value of corridor coverage. The CFO and CEO made the final decision because moving financial volume from established banks carried business-continuity and control risk. Each stakeholder received the case in the language of their own outcome.

**How do you know this was a champion rather than a friendly contact?**

> The Head of Finance had ownership of the problem, helped us navigate the buying group and supported a controlled path to action. The test was whether the contact could mobilize the internal process, not whether they liked the product.

### 9. Proof And Objections

**What did the buyer need to believe, and what was the hardest objection?**

> The buyer needed confidence that shifting volume from trusted banks would not create operational or control risk. I did not ask for a full migration. We used FX as the wedge, started with controlled volume and proved product and support reliability. That proof step addressed the buyer's actual risk and gave the champion evidence to support further adoption.

**Why did you win?**

> We connected a specific corridor thesis to a low-risk way to validate it. The buyer did not have to accept a broad platform promise; they could test an important workflow, see reliable execution and then expand based on evidence.

### 10. Business Case

**How did you connect the product to business value?**

> The finance case combined FX economics, transfer reliability, reconciliation and control. The operations case was fewer urgent payment failures during live tours. The commercial case was broader corridor coverage and the ability to serve more destinations. The executive case was achieving those benefits without introducing unacceptable banking or business-continuity risk. That made the platform relevant to revenue enablement and operating resilience, not just payment cost.

### 11. Commercial Process And Close

**How did you manage negotiation and the close?**

> I owned the process from discovery and positioning through commercial negotiation and signature. The key commercial choice was scope: land with FX and controlled volume rather than force a full-platform migration before trust existed. I kept the next step tied to a concrete decision—expected volume, implementation requirements, rollout ownership or executive approval. The agreement was signed approximately two months after first contact.

### 12. Onboarding And Expansion

## Core Sales-Acumen Answers

### How Do You Build A Prospecting Plan?

> I start with a narrow market thesis rather than a generic account list. I define the ICP and anti-ICP, identify the workflow pain that makes the segment attractive, map a finite account universe and tier it by potential, timing and proof proximity. Then I map the likely buying group, write persona-specific hypotheses and test the message manually before scaling anything. Every reply, meeting and objection updates the account rules and messaging. That is how I built the travel motion at Airwallex: evidence from mature markets, local validation, direct outreach, closed-won learning and then a repeatable vertical playbook.

### How Do You Turn A Trigger Into A Non-Obvious Message?

> I treat a trigger as a routing signal, not as the message. If everyone can see that a company hired a leader, raised funding or announced an expansion, repeating it adds no value. The trigger tells me where to investigate. I then look for the inherited workflow, commercial constraint, stakeholder pressure or missing capability that the buyer may not have fully framed yet. The message should contain that deeper account insight and show how we can help with what the trigger implies—not congratulate them for something they already know.

**Boxo example:**

> UNA's eSIM expansion signal told me where to pay attention. I went deeper by identifying and speaking with relevant eSIM providers before contacting the account. My message to the commercial stakeholder was therefore about solving the partner-supply and launch problem they had inherited, not telling them I had seen their announcement.

**Airwallex example:**

> EU Holidays being a travel agency was not the message. I reviewed the tour groups and destinations they marketed, mapped those destinations to the payment corridors they needed, and identified where Airwallex had stronger rails and competitors were weak. The message was about improving a specific payment workflow during difficult corridors and seasonal peaks—not saying, “I see you run tours.”

### How Do You Tier Accounts?

> I score accounts across four dimensions: potential value, strength of the workflow pain, evidence of timing and our ability to reach the right stakeholders. Tier 1 accounts have high value, a credible reason now and enough evidence to justify manual research with the AD. Tier 2 accounts fit the ICP but have weaker timing or access, so they receive targeted sequencing and monitoring. Tier 3 accounts lack a clear workflow, owner or urgency and stay in nurture. The point is not to create a perfect score; it is to concentrate human effort where a real opportunity is most likely.

### How Do You Run First Discovery?

> I want to understand six things: the business priority, the current workflow, the cost or risk of leaving it unchanged, who owns the problem, how a decision would be made and what a credible next step looks like. I do not begin by presenting every product capability. I use the account hypothesis to earn the conversation, then test whether the problem is real, important and actionable. I leave with a stakeholder map, qualification gaps and a mutually understood next step—or I slow the opportunity down.

### How Do You Qualify An Opportunity?

> I qualify for problem, priority, people, process and proof. Is there a painful workflow tied to a business priority? Is there a real owner or champion? Can we map the technical, risk and economic stakeholders? Is there a decision path and reason to act? And can we define a small proof step with a measurable outcome? Interest without those elements is not yet a qualified enterprise opportunity.

### How Do You Know A Deal Has Actually Advanced?

> I look for a change in buyer evidence, not a change in the CRM label. Has the buyer corrected the problem statement, introduced a required stakeholder, agreed on proof criteria, named the internal decision meeting, clarified a blocker or committed to a dated next step? A demo, proposal or friendly meeting can happen without progress. I only advance the opportunity when the buyer has done something that makes the internal decision more likely or more legible.

### How Do You Build A Business Case?

> I build it throughout the sale in the customer's language. I capture the current workflow, root problem, consequence of inaction, desired outcome, supporting evidence, implementation burden, risk and commercial investment. Then I make it usable by the champion: concise enough to forward, specific enough for finance and clear enough that each stakeholder can defend why now. If the buyer will not correct or contribute to the case, I treat that as a qualification signal.

### What Makes A Real Champion?

> A real champion has three things: personal or organizational incentive, access to the buying process and willingness to take action when I am not in the room. I test that through behavior. Will they share internal context, help map stakeholders, correct my assumptions, arrange the next meeting and explain how the decision gets made? Enthusiasm alone is not champion behavior.

### How Do You Multi-Thread Without Going Around The Champion?

> I make the stakeholder map part of the shared plan with the champion. I explain that different people need different proof and ask for their guidance on sequencing. Then I tailor the conversation: business value for the executive, workflow fit for the owner, feasibility for technical stakeholders and risk controls for legal or security. Multi-threading should make the champion safer and more effective, not surprise or undermine them.

### How Do You Handle Build Versus Buy?

> I first respect that internal build may be rational. Then I diagnose what the customer is really comparing: feature control, speed, talent, maintenance, integration effort, governance and opportunity cost. At Boxo, I did not argue that engineering could not build miniapp infrastructure. I reframed the decision around how quickly they could launch multiple partner services, who would maintain the integration layer and what other roadmap work would be displaced. The right answer may be buy, build or coexist—but the commercial value has to be tied to the buyer's actual constraints.

### How Do You Use A Pilot Or Controlled Rollout?

> A pilot should prove a decision criterion, not postpone a decision. Before it starts, I align on the workflow, success measure, owner, duration, required data or integration, and what happens if the result is positive. At EU Holidays, controlled FX volume reduced the risk of moving away from trusted banks and let us prove reliability before expansion. If there is no meaningful success criterion or post-pilot decision, the pilot is probably activity rather than progress.

### How Do You Handle A Stalled Deal?

> I do not respond by sending more generic follow-ups. I diagnose which condition changed: priority, champion strength, stakeholder coverage, proof, budget, risk or timing. Then I make one clear move—rebuild the business case, secure the missing stakeholder, narrow the proof step, resolve a specific objection or mutually agree to pause. A clean disqualification is better than pretending activity equals progress.

### How Do You Respond When Behind Target?

> I separate coverage from conversion. First I inspect whether I have enough qualified accounts and activity in the right segments. Then I examine conversion by stage: message to reply, reply to meeting, meeting to qualified opportunity and opportunity progression. I identify the largest constraint, change one or two variables and review the result weekly. I do not solve a quality problem by flooding the market with generic activity.

### How Do You Forecast A Deal?

> I forecast from completed buyer milestones and unresolved risk. I look at champion strength, economic-buyer access, stakeholder coverage, validated business case, technical proof, commercial alignment, procurement ownership and the next internal decision date. I separate what the buyer has confirmed from what I am inferring, then name the event that must occur for the forecast to hold. If that event slips or the champion cannot explain the internal process, the forecast changes.

### How Do You Think About Discounting?

> I do not use discounting to create value that is not there. First I test whether the objection is genuinely price or whether it is risk, priority, proof or procurement. If a concession is justified, I trade rather than give: commitment, volume, term, referenceability, faster signature or a narrower scope. The commercial structure should preserve mutual value and a credible expansion path.

### How Do You Handle Procurement Without Losing The Business Sponsor?

> I map procurement, legal, security and finance early enough that they do not appear as imaginary future steps. Once they are active, I keep the business sponsor engaged because forms do not carry urgency. Every blocker needs an owner, and every concession needs a give-get. I continue to restate why the business cares, what date matters and what happens if the process stalls, while respecting the specialist process each team must run.

## Working With An Account Director

### What Does Good AD Partnership Look Like?

> My job is to increase the Account Director's judgment density. Before outreach, I align on account tier, whitespace, stakeholder hypothesis and what would make the opportunity worth the AD's time. During development, I capture the workflow, business priority, champion strength, stakeholder map, competitive context, risk and next step. I bring the AD in when their seniority, commercial judgment or account relationship materially advances the deal—not simply because a meeting exists. After handoff, I stay useful through stakeholder mapping, follow-up and account context without creating confusion about ownership.

### What Makes An Opportunity AD-Ready?

> The AD should know why this account, why now, which workflow, who owns it, who else matters, what evidence supports the opportunity, what could kill it and what the next meeting must accomplish. A calendar invite without that context is not a qualified handoff.

### Example Handoff

> “EU Holidays is a Singapore mid-market travel account. The Head of Finance is the champion, with operations and commercial engaged; the CFO and CEO are final decision-makers. The urgent workflow is overseas payments during seasonal peaks, where delays can disrupt live tours. We have validated FX as the initial wedge. The next meeting needs to confirm expected volume, implementation requirements and the controlled rollout plan. The main risk is confidence in moving volume from existing banks.”
